\chapter{Introduction}
\label{ch:intro}

\section{Context}
\label{sec:intro:object}

A prediction market trades contracts whose payoff depends on the outcome of a future
event, and the price of a contract that pays one dollar if the event occurs can be read
as the market's probability for that event.  Prediction markets have been studied for
decades as aggregators of dispersed information \cite{wolfers2004prediction,arrow2008promise},
almost always through their prices at a daily or coarser resolution.  A decentralised
prediction market is a different kind of object for a data scientist.  On Polymarket,
the venue studied in this thesis, fifteen to twenty thousand markets on politics, sports,
cryptocurrency prices, weather and culture are traded on any given day; each is traded
through a limit order book whose every update is published, and every trade is settled
on a public blockchain under a persistent wallet address.  What the data contain is
therefore a complete market-microstructure record, the book at every price level and
every fill, \emph{and} a persistent pseudonymous identifier of every counterparty, at a
resolution of about two
seconds, across contracts that range from five-minute bets on the next move of Bitcoin to
months-long political questions.  \Cref{fig:intro:graph} shows what three weeks of that
object look like when every wallet is joined by an edge to every market it traded.

This thesis treats that object as a data-analysis and data-mining problem.  The question
is not whether one particular model can be made to work but what the price process of a
prediction market looks like, how its trading is organised, which part of the publicly
observable information predicts the direction of a price over the next minute, hour or
half day, and which families of models are able to read that information.

\section{The problem and why it is difficult}
\label{sec:intro:difficulty}

The prediction task of the thesis is the direction of the mid price of a market (the
average of its best bid and best ask), down, unchanged or up, over a horizon from one minute to twelve hours, at every moment at which
the market's book changes.

Four properties of the object make the task hard.  First, a market price is designed to
\emph{consolidate} information: whatever is publicly known about the event is already in
the price, so what remains to be predicted at short horizons is the mechanics of a thin
order book rather than knowledge of the event.  Second, the price is \emph{bounded and
discrete}: it lives between zero and one and moves in ticks of a thousandth on most
markets, a large share
of the markets sit within a few cents of a boundary, and most of its changes are one-tick
oscillations that revert.  Third, the price is \emph{mostly still}: over one hour it does not
move at all for more than half of the samples, over one minute for nine in ten,
so the task is as much ``will it move'' as ``which way''.  Fourth, much of what moves a
price is \emph{outside the data}: the spot price of Bitcoin for the crypto contracts, the
score of the game for in-play sports, the headline for politics; the order book shows their
effect, not their cause.  Against these, the data offer two things that most financial
datasets do not: the complete book rather than its top, and a persistent pseudonymous
identifier of every counterparty.  Whether either is worth anything for prediction is the empirical question,
and each of the four properties is measured in \cref{ch:data} before any model meets it.

\section{Research questions}
\label{sec:intro:questions}

We ask four questions and answer each of them with a measurement.

\begin{enumerate}[nosep,label=\textbf{Q\arabic*},ref=Q\arabic*]
  \item \label{q:process} \textbf{What regularities does the price process of a prediction
  market have, and how is its trading organised?}  Which share of the price changes is
  information and which is noise, what the price level and the kind of contract do to the
  dynamics, whether related contracts lead one another, how the wallets and the markets are
  connected, and what structure the observable features have (\cref{ch:exploration}).
  \item \label{q:information} \textbf{Which publicly observable information predicts the
  direction of the mid price?}  The market's own price history, the shape of its book, its
  activity, the on-chain trades, the wallets behind them, the contracts of the same event,
  the news flow, the text of the question, and the forecast of a pretrained time-series
  model (\cref{ch:stateless,ch:graph,ch:external}).
  \item \label{q:models} \textbf{Which families of models can read that information, and
  what is a sequential state or a neighbourhood worth over a stateless learner on the same
  inputs?}  Causal rules, gradient-boosted trees and tabular networks, a continuous-time
  stateful model of the order-book stream (a model that keeps a memory per market), and
  message passing on the wallet--market graph (a network that updates each node from its
  neighbours) (\cref{ch:stateless,ch:stateful,ch:graph}).
  \item \label{q:horizon} \textbf{How do the answers change with the horizon}, from one
  minute to twelve hours, and with the kind of market (\cref{ch:horizons})?
\end{enumerate}

\Cref{sec:meth:criteria} fixes what counts as an answer.

\section{Approach}
\label{sec:intro:approach}

The study proceeds in four steps, which are also the four chapters that follow the
background.  The data are
collected from public sources, audited and prepared into a benchmark in which no input can
see the future, and they are explored before any model is trained, so that the models are
held to regularities measured in advance (\cref{ch:data}).  Five families of approaches are
built under explicit rules of inclusion, from the simplest rule on the price to models that
keep a state per market or read the graph of who trades what (\cref{ch:models}).  They are
compared on the same data and under the same protocol, each against the best simpler
approach, and the comparison is repeated at every horizon and on a period kept aside for
the final numbers (\cref{ch:experiments}).  The conclusions say where short-horizon
predictability lives in a prediction market and what a model of it should be made of
(\cref{ch:conclusions}).

\section{Contributions}
\label{sec:intro:contributions}

\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item A description of Polymarket as a data source, from the mechanics of the market
  (outcome tokens, the hybrid order book, settlement, resolution) to the collection and
  audit of a three-week dataset of order books, on-chain fills and market metadata
  (\cref{ch:pm,ch:data}).
  \item An exploratory characterisation of the price process: the event clock (time
  counted in book updates rather than in seconds), the weight of the price level, the
  activity clock (how recently and how often the price has moved) as the main regularity,
  the role of the contract type, the co-movement of sibling contracts (the contracts of
  the same event) without either leading the other, and the persistence of wallet skill
  (\cref{sec:expl:price}).
  \item A characterisation of the wallet--market transaction graph as an object: its
  size, density, degree distributions, single connected component and dense core, its
  growth, a large-graph map of it organised by category, contract type and date, and the
  coordinated publics of single markets (\cref{sec:expl:graph}).
  \item A data-mining study of the feature space: its dimensionality, its organisation by
  market and activity rather than by label, the migration of the information from the
  activity clock to the shape of the book as the horizon grows, and families of markets
  that cut across the categories (\cref{ch:structure}).
  \item A preparation of the data into a benchmark in which no input can see the future,
  with baselines that use only the past, confidence intervals obtained by resampling whole
  hours, a test period kept aside and used once, and a calibration rule (two decision
  thresholds fitted the same way for every model that is compared) (\cref{ch:prep}), and
  the comparison on it of five model families at horizons from one minute to twelve hours
  (\cref{ch:stateless,ch:stateful,ch:graph,ch:external,ch:horizons}).
  \item A continuous-time stateful model with a memory updated at three speeds (every
  book event, every minute, every hour), an order-book encoder that reads each book
  quantity as its own time series before mixing them, and a direct linear path from the
  recent price changes to the output that contains the mean-reversion rule as a special
  case, and the measurement of what its state is worth over the best stateless learner on
  the same inputs: a loss of one to two points at one hour that disappears when the trees
  are its base, a small, significant gain at one minute, alone and on top of the trees,
  and a smaller one on top of the trees at fifteen minutes (\cref{ch:stateful,ch:horizons}).
  \item A per-family and per-horizon account of where short-horizon predictability lives
  in a prediction market and of why each family can or cannot reach it
  (\cref{ch:horizons,ch:conclusions}).
\end{itemize}

\section{Structure of the thesis}
\label{sec:intro:structure}

\Cref{ch:background} gives the background: how a prediction market and Polymarket work,
the notions of time series, graphs, learners and evaluation used in the following chapters,
and the related work.  \Cref{ch:data} follows the data from the three sources
through the preprocessing to the final dataset and closes with its exploratory and
statistical analysis, as a price process, a transaction graph and a feature space.
\Cref{ch:models} is the methodology: the objective, the criteria and the rules that
decided which approaches were included, and the five families of approaches with their
advantages and limits.  \Cref{ch:experiments} gives the experimental setup, the
main results across the families and the horizons, then the results of each family, the
reading across horizons and kinds of market, the locked test set and an economic reading of
the direction calls.
\Cref{ch:conclusions} answers the four questions, states the limits of the study and what
a deployment would need, and discusses the future work.  The appendices list the features,
the run configurations, the steps that reproduce the numbers and the detailed results and
ablations; a glossary closes the thesis.

\paragraph{Code and data.}  Everything in the thesis is produced by one code base: a
Python package (\texttt{polymarket\_gnn}) with the data pipeline in DuckDB and NumPy and
the models in PyTorch, the collection and preprocessing scripts, the audit, graph and
comparison scripts, a test suite for the label construction and the feature indices, and
the reports from which every table is taken.  The code is available at
\url{https://github.com/Asventalis/polymarket-thesis}, in the \path{code/} directory of
the repository that also holds the source of this thesis.  The data are not in the
repository: the raw sources are public (\cref{sec:data:collection}) and the derived
datasets and the trained checkpoints, about 55~GB, are not distributed with the code.
\Cref{app:runs,app:reproduction} give the run labels, the configurations and the steps
that reproduce each number; the raw data are never modified and every derived dataset is
rebuilt from them.

\paragraph{Use of AI tools.}  AI-based assistants were used as tools in this work, for
writing and debugging code and in the preparation of the manuscript, for typesetting,
editing and the correction of spelling and language.  They took no part in the decisions: the
questions, the design of the study, the choice of the data, of the models and of the
experiments, and the analysis and interpretation of the results are our own.
