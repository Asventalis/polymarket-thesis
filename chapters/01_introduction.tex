\chapter{Introduction}
\label{ch:intro}

\section{Context}
\label{sec:intro:object}

A prediction market trades contracts whose payoff depends on the outcome of a future
event, and the price of a contract that pays one dollar if the event occurs can be read
as the market's probability for that event.  Prediction markets have been studied for
decades as aggregators of dispersed information \cite{wolfers2004prediction,arrow2008promise},
almost always through their prices at a daily or coarser resolution.  A decentralised
prediction market is a different kind of object for a data scientist.  On Polymarket,
the venue studied in this thesis, tens of thousands of markets on politics, sports,
cryptocurrency prices, weather and culture are open at the same time; each is traded
through a limit order book whose every update is published, and every trade is settled
on a public blockchain under a persistent wallet address.  What the data contain is
therefore a complete market-microstructure record, the book at every price level and
every fill, \emph{and} the identity of every counterparty, at a resolution of about two
seconds, across contracts that range from five-minute bets on the next move of Bitcoin to
months-long political questions.  \Cref{fig:intro:graph} shows what three weeks of that
object look like when every trade is drawn as an edge between the wallet that made it and
the market it was made on.

This thesis treats that object as a data-analysis and data-mining problem.  The question
is not whether one particular model can be made to work but what the price process of a
prediction market looks like, how its trading is organised, which of the publicly
observable information predicts the direction of a price over the next minute, hour or
half day, and which families of models are able to read that information.  The answers are
empirical and they are given with their intervals.

\section{The problem and why it is difficult}
\label{sec:intro:difficulty}

The prediction task of the thesis is the direction of the mid price of a market, down,
unchanged or up, over a horizon from one minute to twelve hours, at every moment at which
the market's book changes.

Four properties of the object make the task hard, and each of them is measured in the
thesis before any model meets it.  First, a market price is designed to
\emph{consolidate} information: whatever is publicly known about the event is already in
the price, and in its weak form the efficient-market hypothesis says that the past of the
price carries nothing about its future (\cref{sec:bg:lob}); what remains at short horizons
is microstructure, the mechanics of a thin book, not knowledge of the event.  Second, the
price is \emph{bounded and discrete}: it lives in $[0, 1]$ with a tick of a thousandth,
four samples in ten (a \emph{sample} is one market at one moment at which a prediction
is asked, \cref{sec:bg:notation}) sit within five cents of a boundary, and most of its changes are
one-tick oscillations that revert (\cref{sec:expl:level}).  Third, the price is
\emph{mostly still}: over one hour the mid of more than half of the samples does not move
at all, over one minute nine in ten, so the task is as much ``will it move'' as ``which
way'' (\cref{sec:expl:classes}), and a classifier is judged as much by where it puts its
thresholds as by what it knows.  Fourth, much of what moves a price is \emph{outside the
data}: the spot price of Bitcoin for the crypto contracts, the score of the game for
in-play sports, the headline for politics; the order book shows their effect, not their
cause.  Against these, the data offer two things that most financial datasets do not: the
complete book rather than its top, and the identity of every counterparty.  Whether either
is worth anything for prediction is the empirical question.

\section{Research questions}
\label{sec:intro:questions}

The thesis asks four questions and answers each of them with a measurement.

\begin{enumerate}[nosep,label=\textbf{Q\arabic*},ref=Q\arabic*]
  \item \label{q:process} \textbf{What regularities does the price process of a prediction
  market have, and how is its trading organised?}  Which share of the price changes is
  information and which is noise, what the price level and the kind of contract do to the
  dynamics, whether related contracts lead one another, how the wallets and the markets are
  connected, and what structure the observable features have (\cref{ch:exploration}).
  \item \label{q:information} \textbf{Which publicly observable information predicts the
  direction of the mid price?}  The market's own price history, the shape of its book, its
  activity, the on-chain trades, the wallets behind them, the contracts of the same event,
  the news flow, the text of the question, and the forecast of a pretrained time-series
  model (\cref{ch:stateless,ch:graph,ch:external}).
  \item \label{q:models} \textbf{Which families of models can read that information, and
  what is a sequential state or a neighbourhood worth over a stateless learner on the same
  inputs?}  Causal rules, gradient-boosted trees and tabular networks, a continuous-time
  stateful model of the order-book stream, and message passing on the wallet--market graph
  (\cref{ch:stateless,ch:stateful,ch:graph}).
  \item \label{q:horizon} \textbf{How do the answers change with the horizon}, from one
  minute to twelve hours, and with the kind of market (\cref{ch:horizons})?
\end{enumerate}

What counts as an answer, the same samples, the same post-hoc calibration, an interval
that excludes zero, a locked test period, is fixed in \cref{sec:meth:criteria}.

\section{Approach}
\label{sec:intro:approach}

The study proceeds in four steps, which are also the chapters of the thesis.  Twenty-one
days of Polymarket were collected from three public sources, audited and prepared into a
leakage-free dataset with a three-class label, and read with the tools of exploratory
analysis and classical data mining before any model was trained (\cref{ch:data}).  Five
families of approaches were built under explicit rules of inclusion and comparison, from
causal rules on the price to a continuous-time model with message passing on the
wallet--market graph (\cref{ch:models}).  They were compared on the same samples at five
horizons, with hour-block bootstrap intervals and the same calibration for every model,
against the best rule and the best stateless learner on the data (\cref{ch:experiments}).
The conclusions say where short-horizon predictability lives in a prediction market and
what a model of it should be made of (\cref{ch:conclusions}).

\section{Contributions}
\label{sec:intro:contributions}

\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item A description of Polymarket as a data source, from the mechanics of the market
  (outcome tokens, the hybrid order book, settlement, resolution) to the collection and
  audit of a three-week dataset of order books, on-chain fills and market metadata
  (\cref{ch:pm,ch:data}).
  \item An exploratory characterisation of the price process: the event clock, the
  weight of the price level, the activity clock as the main regularity, the role of the
  contract type, the co-movement of sibling contracts without lead or lag, and the
  persistence of wallet skill (\cref{sec:expl:price}).
  \item A characterisation of the wallet--market transaction graph as an object: its
  size, density, degree distributions, single connected component and dense core, its
  growth, a large-graph map of it organised by category, contract type and date, and the
  coordinated publics of single markets (\cref{sec:expl:graph}).
  \item A data-mining study of the feature space: its dimensionality, its organisation by
  market and activity rather than by label, the migration of the information from the
  activity clock to the shape of the book as the horizon grows, and families of markets
  that cut across the categories (\cref{ch:structure}).
  \item A leakage-free preparation of the data into a benchmark with causal baselines,
  hour-block bootstrap intervals, a locked test period and a calibration rule applied to
  every model (\cref{ch:prep}), and the comparison on it of five model families at five
  horizons from one minute to twelve hours
  (\cref{ch:stateless,ch:stateful,ch:graph,ch:external,ch:horizons}).
  \item A continuous-time stateful model with a three-rate hierarchical memory, a
  time-first order-book encoder and a direct path that contains the mean-reversion rule
  as a special case, and the measurement of what its state is worth over the best
  stateless learner on the same inputs: nothing at one hour, a small, significant gain
  at one minute (\cref{ch:stateful,ch:horizons}).
  \item A per-family and per-horizon account of where short-horizon predictability lives
  in a prediction market and of why each family can or cannot reach it
  (\cref{ch:horizons,ch:conclusions}).
\end{itemize}

\section{Structure of the thesis}
\label{sec:intro:structure}

\Cref{ch:background} gives the background: how a prediction market and Polymarket work,
what is known about Polymarket, and the notions of time series, graphs, learners and
evaluation that the thesis uses.  \Cref{ch:data} follows the data from the three sources
through the preprocessing to the final dataset and closes with its exploratory and
statistical analysis, as a price process, a transaction graph and a feature space.
\Cref{ch:models} is the methodology: the objective, the criteria and the rules that
decided which approaches were included, and the five families of approaches with their
advantages and limits.  \Cref{ch:experiments} gives the experimental setup, the
experiments and the results, family by family and then across horizons, kinds of market
and seeds, and discusses them against the four questions.  \Cref{ch:conclusions} draws
the conclusions, states the limits of the study and what a deployment would need, and
lists the future work.  The appendices list the features, the run configurations and the
steps that reproduce the numbers; a glossary closes the thesis.

\paragraph{Code and data.}  Everything in the thesis is produced by one repository: a
Python package (\texttt{polymarket\_gnn}) with the data pipeline in DuckDB and NumPy and
the models in PyTorch, the collection and preprocessing scripts, the audit, graph and
comparison scripts, a test suite for the label construction and the feature indices, and
the reports from which every table is taken.  \Cref{app:runs,app:reproduction} give the
run labels, the configurations and the steps that reproduce each number; the raw data are
never modified and every derived dataset is rebuilt from them.
