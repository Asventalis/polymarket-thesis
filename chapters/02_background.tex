\chapter{Background}
\label{ch:background}

This chapter gives the background that the rest of the thesis assumes: what a prediction
market is and how Polymarket, the venue studied here, works (\cref{sec:pm:what,ch:pm}),

(\cref{sec:bg:tabular}); and the evaluation of a classifier under class imbalance and
dependent samples (\cref{sec:bg:eval}).

\section{Prediction markets}
\label{sec:pm:what}

A \emph{prediction market} is a market whose contracts pay according to the outcome of a
future event, built so that the price of a contract can be read as a probability.  The
simplest contract is the \emph{binary} one: it pays one unit if the event happens and
nothing otherwise, so a trader who believes that the event has probability $q$ is willing
to buy below $q$ and to sell above it, and the price at which buyers and sellers meet is a
probability on which the market agrees.  This is what prediction markets were created
for: not to let people bet, but to \emph{aggregate} what many people know or believe
about an uncertain event into one number, the price, with money at stake as the incentive
to be right.  The argument behind them is that of market efficiency: anyone who knows
better than the price can profit by trading against it, and in doing so moves the price
towards what they know, so that the price ends up incorporating the information of
everyone who trades \cite{wolfers2004prediction,arrow2008promise}.

The idea has a history.  The Iowa Electronic Markets have traded contracts on United
States elections since 1988, and their prices the night before an election have been
closer to the result than the final polls in most of the elections they covered
\cite{berg2008iem}.  Under the idealised assumptions of
risk-neutral traders without budget constraints, the price of a binary contract equals the
market's aggregate probability of the event; under weaker assumptions it is still close to
the mean belief of the traders \cite{wolfers2004prediction,manski2006interpreting}.
Surveys of the empirical literature find that market prices are well calibrated and
competitive with polls and expert forecasts, and that they react to news within minutes
\cite{wolfers2004prediction,snowberg2012prediction}.  Two systematic deviations are
documented: a favourite--longshot bias, where unlikely events are over-priced and likely
events under-priced, which grows with the time to expiration \cite{page2013calibrated};
and, more recently on Polymarket itself, the same bias \cite{cardozo2026favorite} together
with a concentration of trading profits in a very small set of accounts
\cite{akey2026whowins}.

\section{Polymarket}
\label{ch:pm}

Polymarket is the venue whose data the thesis studies.  This section describes its
mechanics in the detail that the data require, from outcome tokens and the hybrid order
book to settlement and resolution, and closes with what the literature has found about it.

\subsection{How Polymarket works}
\label{sec:pm:mechanics}

Polymarket is a decentralised prediction market built on the Polygon blockchain, with
the United States dollar stablecoin USDC as collateral.  The unit of trading is the
\emph{market}, a question with exactly two outcomes.  For a binary question the outcomes
are ``Yes'' and ``No''; for the others they are the two sides of the question, ``Up'' and
``Down'' for a price interval, the two teams of a game, ``Over'' and ``Under'' for a total.
An \emph{event} can have more than two possible outcomes, the candidates of an election,
the strikes of a price ladder, the teams of a tournament; on Polymarket such an event is
not one market with many outcomes but a group of two-outcome markets, one per candidate,
whose prices are linked because exactly one of them resolves to ``Yes'' (the
``negative risk'' structure described below).  Each outcome of a market is a token, a
conditional token in the sense of the Conditional Token Framework that Polymarket inherits
from Gnosis: one dollar of collateral can at any time be \emph{split} into one Yes token and
one No token, a pair can be \emph{merged} back into one dollar, and after resolution the
winning token is \emph{redeemed} for one dollar and the losing one for nothing.  The two
prices therefore sum to about one dollar, and a trader who wants to sell Yes at price $p$
is indistinguishable, for the matching engine, from one who wants to buy No at $1 - p$.

Trading takes place on a central limit order book (CLOB) that is \emph{hybrid}.  Orders
are signed messages that the trader submits to Polymarket's off-chain operator, which
keeps the book, publishes its state and every change to it, and matches crossing orders;
a match is then settled on-chain by the exchange contract, which transfers the tokens and
the collateral and emits a fill event with the maker, the taker, the token, the side, the
quantity, the price and the fee.  The book is thus observable as a stream of price-level
updates at the resolution of the operator's clock, and the trades are observable, with a
delay of one block, as on-chain records attributed to wallet addresses.  The two streams
are not the same thing: most book events are orders placed or cancelled away from the best
quotes and never trade; a fill is a book event that found a counterparty.  In a
multi-outcome event, the candidates of an election or the strikes of a ``Bitcoin above $x$''
ladder, the two-outcome markets of the candidates are linked by the payoff structure
(exactly one candidate wins), with a ``negative risk'' conversion that lets a trader hold
the No side of every candidate as one position; the prices of such siblings must respect
bounds that the market does not always enforce \cite{gebele2026arbitrage}.

\subsection{Tokens, contracts and transactions: the blockchain layer}
\label{sec:pm:chain}

Polygon is a proof-of-stake chain that produces a block roughly every two seconds; every
fill is a transaction in one of its blocks and carries the block number, the transaction
hash and the wallet addresses of the maker and the taker.  Because settlement is on-chain,
every trade is attributed to a wallet that persists across markets and across time.  This
makes it possible to build a bipartite graph between wallets and markets, and to ask whether
the history of a wallet (its activity, its past accuracy, the other markets it trades) is
informative about the price of the markets it trades now.  Works on Polymarket have used
wallet-level data to study who wins and who loses \cite{akey2026whowins}, insider trading and
informed flow \cite{bsic2026polymarket}, and arbitrage between related markets
\cite{saguillo2025unravelling}.  None of them, to my knowledge, had used the wallet--market
graph as the input of a forecasting model; the graph models of \cref{ch:graph} do.  The block is also the natural time step of the
on-chain stream and the time step of every stateful model in the thesis
(\cref{sec:data:blocks}); the graph itself is described in \cref{sec:expl:graph}.

\subsection{The limit order book}
\label{sec:pm:lob}

A limit order book \cite{gould2013limit} is, for one token, two sorted lists of resting
limit orders: bids, the prices at which traders are willing to buy, and asks, the prices at
which they are willing to sell, each with the total size resting at that price.  The highest
bid and the lowest ask are the best quotes, their average is the \emph{mid price} and their
difference the \emph{spread}; the size resting at the first few levels on each side is the
\emph{depth}.  Prices on Polymarket live in $[0, 1]$ with a tick of $0.001$ on most markets
and $0.01$ on some, so a contract that is almost certain trades at $0.99$ or above and its
price can only move by a few ticks.  Because the Yes and No books mirror each other (a bid
for Yes at $p$ is an ask for No at $1 - p$), the book of one side describes the market, and
this thesis reads the Yes side.  An \emph{order-book event} is a change of the size resting
at one price on one side, caused by an order arriving, being cancelled or being filled; the
operator publishes it with the new best quotes, the new mid and the new spread.  Most events
do not touch the best quotes: on a typical day about four events in a hundred change the mid
(\cref{sec:expl:stream}).  A \emph{snapshot} is the full state of the book at one moment.

Two consequences of this structure recur in every result of the thesis.  A mid price is a
bounded, discrete process observed through a thin book, so most of its changes are one-tick
oscillations produced by an order arriving at or leaving the best quote, which are not
information and which revert; and the dynamics depend on the price level, since a market at
$0.98$ has two ticks of room on one side and a hundred on the other.
\textcite{dubach2026anatomy} measures this microstructure on a 52-day sample of Polymarket
books and reports three findings that matter here.  The depth of a book depends on how long
the market lives, on its price level and on its volume.  The resting orders are not
concentrated near the best quotes but spread over hundreds of price levels, so the first
levels of the book are thin even in a liquid market.  And the direction of a trade,
whether the taker bought or sold, guessed from the public book agrees with the on-chain
record only about 60\% of the time.  For this reason the thesis reads the direction of
every trade from the on-chain fill, where it is recorded exactly, and not from the book.

\subsection{The life of a market}
\label{sec:pm:life}

A market is created with its question, its resolution rules, its two outcomes and an end
date; its contract is deployed on-chain and it starts accepting orders at known times, both
recorded in the market's metadata and both known in advance.  It then trades until the event
is decided.  Some markets are born and die within minutes: the crypto interval markets ask
whether the price of Bitcoin, Ethereum or Solana will be above its starting value at the end
of a five- or fifteen-minute interval, and a new one opens as the previous one closes; their
prices follow an external reference, the spot price on a centralised exchange, with a lag of
a few hundred milliseconds \cite{young2026openmarket}.  Sports markets trade before and during
the game, with a recorded start time; political, economic and cultural questions trade for
weeks or months; daily temperature and weather questions resolve every day.  During the three
weeks studied here about 175{,}000 markets existed and 15{,}000--21{,}000 were active on any
given day (\cref{ch:data}).

Resolution is not decided by Polymarket.  When the event is over, a user proposes the outcome
to UMA's optimistic oracle and posts a bond in collateral; the proposal stands if nobody
disputes it within a two-hour challenge window, a wrong or premature proposal forfeits the
bond, and a disputed proposal goes through the oracle's dispute-resolution process, a vote of
the holders of its token \cite{polymarket2026resolution}.
The market's metadata records the resolution source named in the question and the end
date of the oracle's process, and the winning token becomes redeemable once the oracle has
ruled.  Resolution is therefore an event that happens after the end date, sometimes well
after it; the thesis never uses the outcome or the resolution time as an input, and it
predicts the price while the market is still open.

\subsection{What is known about Polymarket}
\label{sec:pm:related}

Machine-learning work on Polymarket has so far concentrated on \emph{outcome}
forecasting with language models and news \cite{cheng2026polybench,barot2026polyswarm},
on dataset construction \cite{jia2026unlocking,young2026openmarket} and on the
economics of the platform \cite{reichenbach2025exploring,tsang2026anatomy}.
\textcite{young2026openmarket} is the closest in spirit: a logistic model on 43
order-flow features does not beat the Polymarket mid price itself as a predictor of the
outcome of the 15-minute Bitcoin markets.  The present thesis predicts the
\emph{price}, not the outcome, over horizons at which the market is still open; the
natural references are therefore the limit-order-book forecasting literature
(\cref{sec:bg:lob}) and the literature on dynamic graphs (\cref{sec:bg:dyn}).

\section{Order books, price series and the forecasting task}
\label{sec:bg:ts}
\label{sec:bg:ts:concepts}

A \emph{time series} is a sequence of observations indexed by time.  The series of this
thesis are not sampled on a regular grid: the order book of a market changes whenever an
order arrives, is cancelled or is filled, and a fill happens whenever two orders cross, so
the two raw streams are sequences of timed \emph{events}, hundreds of thousands per market
on a busy day and a handful on a quiet one.  Two ways of reading such a stream recur in
what follows.  The first summarises the past at a chosen time by a fixed set of quantities,
the last price, its change over a few fixed lookbacks, the number of events in the last
minutes, the shape of the book now, and hands the summary to a learner that has no memory
of its own: the \emph{stateless} reading, in which time enters only through the features.
The second keeps a \emph{state} per market, a vector updated at every event or every block
from the new observations, and asks the state for the prediction: the \emph{stateful}
reading, that of recurrent networks.  The two differ in what they can see (a state can in
principle keep the order in which the events happened, a summary cannot), in how they are
trained (a table of summaries can be shuffled and revisited; a state must be trained in
time order through the stream) and in how they handle irregular time (a summary can include
the time since the last event; a state has to be told how much time has elapsed, through a
decay or an explicit input).  The forecasting task itself takes two forms, the regression
of the value at a future horizon $H$ or the classification of the \emph{direction} of its
change; the thesis uses the second, with three classes (\textsc{down}, \textsc{const},
\textsc{up}) and horizons from one minute to twelve hours, and the null model against
which every result is read is the random walk, under which the past of the price says
nothing about its future.

\paragraph{The quantities of a book.}  With $p^{b}_1 > p^{b}_2 > \cdots$ the bid prices,
$p^{a}_1 < p^{a}_2 < \cdots$ the ask prices and $s^{b}_k$, $s^{a}_k$ the sizes resting at
level $k$, the mid, the spread and the depth imbalance over the first $n$ levels are
\begin{equation}
  \operatorname{mid} = \frac{p^{b}_1 + p^{a}_1}{2}, \qquad
  \operatorname{spread} = p^{a}_1 - p^{b}_1, \qquad
  \operatorname{imb}_n = \frac{\sum_{k \le n} s^{b}_k - \sum_{k \le n} s^{a}_k}{\sum_{k \le n} s^{b}_k + \sum_{k \le n} s^{a}_k} \in [-1, 1],
\end{equation}
and the \emph{microprice}, $(p^{b}_1 s^{a}_1 + p^{a}_1 s^{b}_1) / (s^{b}_1 + s^{a}_1)$, a
weighted average of the two best quotes in which each price is weighted by the size resting
on the \emph{opposite} side: when little is resting at the best bid, the bid is the quote
more likely to be consumed next, the weight of the bid price grows and the microprice sits
closer to the bid.  It is the best one-number guess of where the next mid will be.  The \emph{order-flow imbalance} of \textcite{cont2014price}
accumulates, over a span of events, the signed changes of the size at the best quotes:
an order that adds size to the best bid or removes it from the best ask pushes the mid up,
and the sum of such pushes over the recent past is strongly related to the next move of
the mid.  These quantities are the columns of the book vector $\mathbf{b}_i$ of
\cref{sec:data:tensors} and of the hand-made features of \cref{sec:prep:features}.

\paragraph{Price predictability and limit order books.}
\label{sec:bg:lob}
The efficient-market hypothesis in its weak form states that past prices carry no
information about future prices \cite{fama1970efficient}.  At short horizons this is
only approximately true: \textcite{lo1988stock} reject the random-walk model on weekly
stock returns, and the empirical ``stylised facts'' of high-frequency returns
\cite{cont2001empirical} include a negative autocorrelation at the shortest lags
produced by the bid--ask bounce and by the discreteness of prices.  Deep learning on
limit order books started with DeepLOB \cite{zhang2019deeplob}, a convolutional network
followed by a recurrent layer that reads a window of the last hundred book states and
predicts the next move of the mid, on the FI-2010 benchmark of Finnish stocks
\cite{ntakaris2018benchmark}.  Two recent studies are directly relevant.
\textcite{briola2024deep} show that predictability depends on the microstructural
properties of each asset (tick size, spread, depth) more than on the architecture, and
\textcite{berti2025tlob} show that a well-tuned MLP matches transformer models on the
same benchmarks and that the label should be defined with a smoothing window that is
chosen independently of the horizon.  Both findings recur in this thesis: the price
level (a proxy for the effective tick size) is the main driver of the results, and the
simplest architectures are hard to beat.  Three further results from the equity
literature frame the choices of \cref{ch:models}.  \textcite{sirignano2019universal} train
one recurrent model on the books of a thousand stocks and find that it beats models
trained on each stock alone: the predictive relation between book and next move is to a
large extent \emph{universal}, which motivates one model pooled over all Polymarket markets
rather than one per market.  \textcite{kolm2023dofi} compare raw book states with
order-flow representations as inputs of the same networks and find that flows win, and that
the useful horizon is of the order of a few price changes;
\textcite{lucchese2024predictability} confirm on a larger panel that short-horizon
predictability is present almost everywhere and that \emph{what} the model is shown matters
more than \emph{which} model is used.  Two lessons of the general time-series forecasting
literature are also used.  The first is a caution \cite{zeng2023transformers}: on the
standard forecasting benchmarks a one-layer linear model on the raw window matches or
beats several large transformer forecasters, so a complex encoder has to be checked against
a trivial one on the same inputs.  The second is a design pattern called \emph{channel
independence} \cite{nie2023patchtst}: when a series has many variables, reading each
variable as its own sequence with shared weights, and mixing the variables only
afterwards, is more accurate and more robust than mixing them at every step.  The ``time
first'' encoder of \cref{sec:models:encoders} applies this pattern to the order-book
window.

\section{Notation}
\label{sec:bg:notation}

The same symbols are used from here to the end of the thesis, and the glossary repeats
them.

\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item \textbf{Markets, wallets, time.}  $\mathcal{M}$ is the set of markets and
  $\mathcal{W}$ the set of wallets; $m \in \mathcal{M}$ and $w \in \mathcal{W}$ denote one
  of each.  Time $t$ is measured in seconds; a block of the Polygon chain, about two
  seconds, is the time step of the on-chain stream and of the stateful models
  (\cref{sec:data:blocks}).
  \item \textbf{The book stream.}  The order book of market $m$ is observed as a sequence
  of timed events $(t_1, \mathbf{b}_1), (t_2, \mathbf{b}_2), \ldots$ with
  $t_1 < t_2 < \cdots$, where $\mathbf{b}_i \in \mathbb{R}^{52}$ describes the state of
  the book just after the $i$-th event: best quotes, prices and sizes at ten levels,
  spread, mid price and order-flow statistics (\cref{sec:data:tensors}).
  $\operatorname{mid}_m(t)$ is the mid price of the last event of $m$ strictly before
  $t$, a step function of $t$.
  \item \textbf{Horizon and label.}  $H$ is the prediction horizon, one of 60~s, 900~s,
  1~h, 6~h and 12~h.  The label of market $m$ at time $t$ is
  \begin{equation}
    y_{m,t} =
    \begin{cases}
      \textsc{up}    & \text{if } \operatorname{mid}_m(t + H) > \operatorname{mid}_m(t), \\
      \textsc{const} & \text{if } \operatorname{mid}_m(t + H) = \operatorname{mid}_m(t), \\
      \textsc{down}  & \text{if } \operatorname{mid}_m(t + H) < \operatorname{mid}_m(t),
    \end{cases}
    \label{eq:label:bg}
  \end{equation}
  where \textsc{const} means exactly equal: the mid moves in ticks of $0.001$, and a move
  of one tick or more in either direction is \textsc{up} or \textsc{down}
  (\cref{sec:data:labels}).  The classes are indexed $c \in \{1, 2, 3\}$ for
  \textsc{down}, \textsc{const}, \textsc{up}, and $\mathbf{e}_y \in \{0, 1\}^{3}$ is the
  one-hot vector of the label.
  \item \textbf{Samples.}  A \emph{sample} is a pair $(m, t)$ at which a prediction is
  asked: a block $t$ in which market $m$ received at least one book event.  Predictions
  are tied to the book events because an event is the moment at which something new about
  the market is observed; a market whose book did not change in a block has nothing new to
  read, its state is the one of its last event, and a prediction for it would repeat the
  previous one.  Nothing is required of the market at $t + H$: the label reads the last
  mid before $t + H$, whatever happened in between (\cref{sec:data:labels}).  A day has
  about ten million samples.  When the market and the time are not needed, a sample is
  written $(\mathbf{x}_i, y_i)$, $i = 1, \ldots, N$.
  \item \textbf{Lookbacks.}  For a span $L$,
  $\Delta^{-}_{L}(m, t) = \operatorname{mid}_m(t) - \operatorname{mid}_m(t - L)$ is the
  change of the mid over the past $L$; the rules of \cref{sec:models:baselines} read
  nothing else.
  \item \textbf{Models and parameters.}  A learned model is a function $f(\cdot;
  \boldsymbol{\theta})$ of its inputs with parameters $\boldsymbol{\theta}$ (all the
  weights and biases, collected in one vector).  A stateless model reads a vector of
  hand-made features $\mathbf{x}_{m,t} \in \mathbb{R}^{61}$, each a function of the data
  strictly before $t$ (\cref{sec:prep:features}), and returns three class probabilities
  $\hat{\mathbf{p}}_{m,t} = f(\mathbf{x}_{m,t}; \boldsymbol{\theta}) \in \Delta^{3}$, where
  $\Delta^{3}$ is the set of probability vectors over the three classes.
  \item \textbf{Stateful models.}  A stateful model keeps a vector
  $\mathbf{h}_m(t) \in \mathbb{R}^{d}$ per market, with $d = 128$.  Between two events of
  $m$ the state decays, $\mathbf{h}_m(t) = e^{-r (t - t')}\, \mathbf{h}_m(t')$ for
  $t' < t$, with a learned rate $r$; at a block in which $m$ is active the state is
  updated from the window $\mathbf{W}_{m,t} \in \mathbb{R}^{K \times 52}$ of its last $K$
  book states, $\mathbf{h}_m(t) \leftarrow U\bigl(\mathbf{h}_m(t^{-}),
  \phi(\mathbf{W}_{m,t})\bigr)$, where $\phi$ is the window encoder and $U$ a gated
  recurrent update; the prediction is
  $\hat{\mathbf{p}}_{m,t} = g(\mathbf{h}_m(t), \mathbf{x}_{m,t})$
  (\cref{sec:models:ctdgnn}).
  \item \textbf{Graphs.}  A graph $G = (V, E)$ is a set of nodes $V$ and a set of edges
  $E \subseteq V \times V$.  The transaction graph is bipartite,
  $V = \mathcal{W} \cup \mathcal{M}$ with every edge joining a wallet to a market, and
  dynamic: an edge is an event $e = (w, m, t, \mathbf{a}_e)$, a fill of wallet $w$ on
  market $m$ at time $t$ with attributes $\mathbf{a}_e \in \mathbb{R}^{7}$ (side, role,
  size, price, fee, token orientation; \cref{sec:models:mp}).  $E_t$ is the set of edges
  of block $t$ and $N_t(v)$ the edges of $E_t$ incident to node $v$.  Wallets have states
  $\mathbf{h}_w(t) \in \mathbb{R}^{d}$ like markets.
  \item \textbf{Decision and score.}  Every model outputs three logits
  $\mathbf{z}_{m,t} \in \mathbb{R}^{3}$; the probabilities are
  $\hat{\mathbf{p}} = \operatorname{softmax}(\mathbf{z} + \boldsymbol{\delta})$ with an
  offset $\boldsymbol{\delta} = (\delta_{\mathrm{down}}, 0, \delta_{\mathrm{up}})$, and the
  predicted class is $\hat{y} = \arg\max \hat{\mathbf{p}}$.  The offset is the
  \emph{calibration} of the decision rule.  The training data are dominated by
  \textsc{const} and the models are trained with class weights or undersampling, so their
  raw probabilities are tilted and the most probable class is not the one that maximises
  balanced accuracy; two constants added to the \textsc{down} and \textsc{up} logits,
  chosen once on the validation day to maximise balanced accuracy and then frozen, move
  the decision boundary to where the score wants it
  (\cref{sec:bg:learning:imbalance,sec:models:protocol}).  Every model receives its own
  offset, so that models are compared calibrated against calibrated.  The score is balanced accuracy,
  $\mathrm{BA} = \tfrac{1}{3} \sum_{c} \mathrm{recall}_c$, where $\mathrm{recall}_c$ is the
  share of the samples of class $c$ that are predicted as $c$ (\cref{sec:bg:eval}).
\end{itemize}
