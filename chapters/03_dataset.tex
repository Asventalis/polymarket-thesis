\chapter[The Dataset]{The Dataset: Collection, Preparation and Exploration}
\label{ch:data}

This chapter follows the data from their three sources to the objects the models read, in
the order of the pipeline: what was collected and from where (\cref{sec:data:sources,sec:data:collection}),
how large and how reliable it is (\cref{sec:data:scale,sec:data:quality}), how the raw
streams were preprocessed into a common time axis and per-block tensors (\cref{ch:prep}),
and how the final dataset, its labels, samples, features and periods, was defined
(\cref{sec:data:final}).  It closes with an exploratory and statistical analysis of the
data (\cref{ch:exploration}): the price process, the transaction graph and the feature
space, read with the tools of exploratory analysis and classical data mining before any
model is trained.

\section{The four tables}
\label{sec:data:sources}

The dataset covers twenty-one consecutive days, from 6 to 26 March 2026, and consists of
four tables (\cref{tab:data:sources}).  The \emph{markets} table describes every market
that existed in the period: question text, tags, the two outcome tokens, creation and
end dates, and the final outcome.  The \emph{order book} table is the stream of
price-level updates of the ``Yes'' side of every market's book: each row carries the
best bid and ask, the changed price level, size and side, and the mid price, with two
timestamps (creation and reception).  The \emph{snapshots} table holds full order-book
states.  The \emph{transactions} table is the on-chain record of every fill: block
number, timestamp, transaction hash, wallet, role (maker or taker), token, side, quantity,
price and fee.

\section{Collection}
\label{sec:data:collection}

The three streams come from three places, described in the following.

\subsection{Market metadata: the Gamma API}

The \emph{markets} table was built from Polymarket's public metadata service (the Gamma
API): the markets closed in the window were paged through its keyset endpoint, ordered by
start date, with the tag list attached, and the markets that appear in the transactions but
not in that listing (tokens traded in the window on markets created outside it) were
completed one by one through the order-book operator's markets-by-token endpoint.  The tags
are the source of the eight categories used throughout the thesis (crypto, sports, esports,
politics, weather, economy and tech, culture, other), assigned by a fixed mapping from the
tag list.

\subsection{The order-book archive}

The \emph{order book} and \emph{snapshots} tables come from a public archive of the
operator's websocket feed, the \emph{Polymarket Tick-Level Orderbook Dataset} published on
Kaggle by Marvin Gozo under a CC~BY-NC~4.0 licence \cite{gozo2026polymarket}: one file per
day, restricted to the Yes side of every book.  Every row
of the feed carries two timestamps: the time at which the operator created the message and
the time at which the archive's collector received it; we use the creation time as
the time of the event and keep the reception time only to check the feed's latency.

\subsection{The on-chain transactions: a blockchain scraper}

We collected the \emph{transactions} table with a modified version of
\path{poly-trade-scan} \cite{martkir2026polytradescan}, an open-source Python client that
connects to a Polygon node over a WebSocket JSON-RPC endpoint, reads every block, filters
the transactions addressed to Polymarket's exchange contracts and decodes their
\texttt{matchOrders} calls.  The original tool emits one row per transaction, the taker's
order with its nominal amounts; our version decodes the whole call, the amounts actually
filled and received by the taker, the filled amount and the fee of every maker order, and
emits one row per wallet and per fill (block number, timestamp, transaction hash, order
index, wallet, role, token, side, quantity, price, total collateral and fee rate), with
the longer timeouts and the many retries that a free public node requires.  The node was
the public endpoint of dRPC (\path{wss://polygon.drpc.org}); the token identifier of
every fill is then mapped to its market through the markets table.  A transaction with one taker and $n$ makers yields one taker row and $n$
maker rows, so the 219 million rows are \emph{fill legs}; 79 million of them are taker legs.

\section{Scale, coverage and storage}
\label{sec:data:scale}

Daily activity is stable over the twenty-one days of the window, 6--26 March 2026:
9.2--11.8 million transaction rows,
122{,}000--161{,}000 distinct wallets and 15{,}000--21{,}000 markets with at least one fill
per day.  The data were stored as Parquet files and queried with DuckDB
\cite{raasveldt2019duckdb}; all derived tensors were produced with SQL and NumPy and
checked by unit tests.  The raw files are never modified and every derived dataset is
rebuilt from them (\cref{app:reproduction}).

\section{Data quality}
\label{sec:data:quality}

Four properties of the data shape every later decision.

\paragraph{One-sided and crossed books.}  When one side of the book is empty, the
missing price is set to $0.49$ or $0.51$ and the mid is computed from it; 2.4\% of the
rows have such a mid and 2.1\% a crossed book.  Both kinds of row are kept: the results
of \cref{ch:experiments} change by less than $0.2$ points when they are excluded.

\paragraph{Token orientation.}  The order book, and therefore the label, refers to
token~1 only in Yes/No markets and to token~2 in all other markets (Up/Down, Over/Under,
team~A/team~B), whereas the raw transaction direction is defined with respect to token~1
everywhere.  Without a correction, the sign of a buy is wrong relative to the label in 82\%
of the fill legs of a typical day, which fall on the 8.5\% of labelled 1h samples whose
market is not a Yes/No one.  Every model reads the
corrected sign (\cref{sec:models:mp}).

\paragraph{Price level.}  About 38\% of the samples have a mid below $0.05$ or above
$0.95$ (\cref{fig:expl:midhist}); their dynamics are those of a bounded, one-tick process
and they are where every model struggles most.  Every result is therefore reported
separately on the extreme and the central samples (\cref{sec:data:protocol}).

\paragraph{Gaps.}  The activity of the venue has one dip in the window.  Between 01:00
and 03:00 UTC on 20 March the hourly number of book events falls from about nine million
to 1.3 million and the hourly number of fill legs from about 500 thousand to 79 thousand
(\cref{fig:graph:summary} shows the fills); both recover within three hours.  The two streams are
collected independently and show the same dip, so it belongs to the activity of the
venue.  20 March lies between the validation day and the test period
(\cref{sec:data:splits}), and no model is trained or evaluated on it.

\section{Preprocessing}
\label{ch:prep}

The data of \cref{sec:data:sources} are two streams on two clocks.  This section turns
them into the objects the models read: a common time axis and per-block tensors.

\subsection{Time axis: blocks and the synchronisation of the two streams}
\label{sec:data:blocks}

Polygon produces a block roughly every two seconds, and every transaction carries its
block number.  The block is therefore the natural time step of the on-chain stream and
it is also the time step of the models: a ``step'' is one block, the set of transactions
in it, and the order-book updates that arrived since the previous block.  About 1{,}800
blocks per hour contain Polymarket transactions.  Order-book updates arrive at their own
times, on a different clock (\cref{sec:data:sources}): every stateful model reads the book
updates created strictly before a block time and the fills of the block itself, so a
sample at block $t$ never sees an update created at or after $t$.

\subsection{Derived tensors}
\label{sec:data:tensors}

\paragraph{Order-book features.}  For every market, one row is written every twenty
order-book events from the book state at that moment: a vector of 52 numbers.  Forty of
them describe ten levels on each side, the price of each level relative to the mid,
$(p - \mathrm{mid})/\mathrm{mid}$, and the size resting there (zero where the level is
empty); the other twelve are the mid price (column 40), the spread, the best bid and ask,
and eight quantities accumulated over the twenty events of the row: the order-flow imbalance
of \textcite{cont2014price}, the total size changed, the number of events, the share of
events on the bid side, the share of size removals, the mean depth of the first ten levels
on each side and the asymmetry between the two.  The rows are stored as hourly Parquet
partitions (about 287{,}000 rows per hour, 27~GB in total).  To these, the models append
the long-lookback price features of \cref{sec:models:lookback} and the other constant
columns of \cref{sec:models:ctdgnn}.


\paragraph{Market lifecycle.}  A table of 283{,}318 open/close events gives, for every
market, the time at which its contract was deployed (known in advance, hence causal)
and the time of its last trade (known only in hindsight).  The deployment time defines
when a market may enter the model.  The last-trade time is used only to define the
evaluation sample (a label is not computed when the market stops trading before the
horizon), never as an input.

\paragraph{Static market features.}  Each market has a 390-dimensional static vector:
a 384-dimensional sentence embedding of its question, description and tags, produced by a
frozen MiniLM sentence encoder \cite{reimers2019sbert}, and six numbers that encode the
hour, the weekday and the month of its creation as sines and cosines.  These features are
fixed at market creation, so they are leak-free; they initialise the market's state
(\cref{sec:models:state}) through a fixed random projection.  What the text contributes
to prediction is measured in \cref{sec:external:text}.

\paragraph{Identifiers.}  Markets and wallets are mapped to dense indices
(175{,}292 distinct markets, 646{,}394 wallets) so that their states can live in two matrices on
the GPU.

\section{The final dataset: labels, samples, features and periods}
\label{sec:data:final}

This section defines the dataset on which every model of the thesis is trained and
evaluated: the label and the sample population, the engineered features and the other
feature tables, and the split of the twenty-one days into training, validation and test
periods.

\subsection{Labels}
\label{sec:data:labels}

The target of a market $m$ at block time $t$ and horizon $H$ is the direction of the
change of its mid price between $t$ and $t + H$, the label $y_{m,t}$ of
\cref{eq:label:bg}, where $\operatorname{mid}_m(s)$ is the mid price of the last
order-book update of $m$ strictly before $s$.  A price is \textsc{const} when the two mids are \emph{exactly}
equal: there is no tolerance band, the quotes move in ticks of $0.001$ so the mid moves in
steps of half a tick or more, and any move in either direction is \textsc{up} or \textsc{down}.  Three choices matter:

\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item \textbf{Three classes.}  The price is a step function with ticks of $0.001$;
  over one hour the mid of more than half of the samples does not move at all
  (\cref{tab:expl:classes}).  Collapsing ``no change'' into one of the two directions
  would make the task ill-posed; the three-class formulation keeps it well posed.
  \item \textbf{The last update before $t+H$, wherever it is.}  The update that defines
  $\operatorname{mid}_m(t+H)$ can live in any hour between $t$ and $t+H$, so the lookup
  scans every hour of the interval: a market that moved in between and was quiet
  afterwards is labelled by that move, not \textsc{const}.
  \item \textbf{Unknown is unknown.}  If $t+H$ is beyond the loaded data, the label is
  missing, not ``whatever the last price was''.
\end{itemize}


\paragraph{Samples.}  A sample is a (market, block) pair $(m, t)$ in which the market
received at least one order-book update in the block (\cref{sec:bg:notation}): a
prediction is asked whenever something new about a market is observed, and a market whose
book did not change in a block has nothing new to read.  Every model is asked for a
prediction at every such pair, so markets that update often contribute many samples.  Because consecutive
samples of the same market within a few seconds are almost identical, every result is
also reported on a \emph{deduplicated} subset that keeps at most one sample per market
per ten minutes.  At the 6h horizon the validation day has 9.3 million samples, 1.2
million after deduplication.

\subsection{Feature engineering}
\label{sec:prep:features}

\paragraph{The sixty-one engineered features.}  For every tensor row, 61 causal features are computed from the clean data: the mid, its changes over
15 minutes and 1, 3, 6 and 12 hours and their signs, and the hour (\emph{price history},
the inputs of the direct path); the spread, the level-1, 5-level and 10-level depth
imbalances, the microprice, the sizes and the number of filled levels (\emph{book
shape}); the order-flow accumulators, event counts in the last 1, 5, 15 and 60 minutes,
the time since the last snapshot and since the last mid change, the number of mid changes
and the realised volatility of the last hour, and the position of the mid in its one-hour
range (\emph{activity and clock}); the signed taker flow of the on-chain trades over 5, 15
and 60 minutes (\emph{trades}); and the category, the outcome type, the time to the end
date, the duration and the age of the market (\emph{static}).  The full list, with
the exact definition of each feature, is in \cref{app:features}.  These features feed the
stateless learners of \cref{sec:models:stateless} and, from \cref{sec:models:handinputs}
on, the stateful model as well; \cref{ch:structure} studies their structure before any
learner is trained on them.

\paragraph{Sibling features.}  The contracts of the same event form a \emph{market--market} graph; its information is
measured through ten causal \emph{sibling features} computed for every row from
the last rows of the market's siblings within six hours: how many are active, their mean
15-minute and 1-hour returns, the largest of the latter, the sum of their mid prices (the
consistency of the event), the time since the latest sibling move, their net trade flow and
order-flow sign.  Siblings are the contracts of the same game (from the market's slug) or of
the same question template and end date (the candidates of one election, the strikes of one
ladder, the two sides of one seat): 9{,}356 groups of two to sixty markets, covering 74\% of
the rows of the one-hour feature table with about eleven active siblings each.  If the trees find nothing in these
features, a message-passing layer over the same edges has nothing to carry, and the
lead--lag statistic of \cref{sec:expl:siblings} says why.

\paragraph{News features.}  The \emph{news flow}: every market is mapped to the entities named in its question and tags
(proper-noun phrases with generic words removed; 129 thousand of the 175 thousand markets,
74\% of the validation samples), and the GDELT Global Knowledge Graph files, one every fifteen
minutes, give for each entity the number of articles naming it and their tone; twelve causal
features per row follow (counts over one, six and twenty-four hours, bursts against the
previous seven days, tone and its change, share of the news flow, time since the last
mention), computed from the files published at least ten minutes before the row.  Like the sibling features, the news features
are measured in the stateless learner first (\cref{sec:external:news}).

\paragraph{Wallet features.}  For the graph models, every transaction edge carries a
seven-dimensional feature (the logarithms of the collateral and of the number of tokens,
the price, the fee rate, buy or sell, the token orientation, maker or taker) and, in
the enriched variants, a causal record of the wallet's past accuracy at the horizon
(``skill''), a placebo skill (another wallet's record), and the wallet's past behaviour
(activity, maker share, one-sidedness, size, breadth); \cref{sec:models:mp} says how they
enter.

\paragraph{Foundation-model features.}\label{sec:models:tsfm}  Eleven numbers are appended
to every window row, derived from a zero-shot Chronos-2 forecast \cite{ansari2025chronos2} of the market's one-minute mid series: the
10\%, 50\% and 90\% quantiles of the forecast at 15, 30 and 60 minutes ahead, expressed
as scaled differences from the current mid, the sign of the 60-minute median change,
and an availability flag.  Forecasts are computed on a five-minute grid from the
512 minutes of history strictly before the grid time, and a sample at time $t$ reads
the last forecast made at most eight minutes before $t$; relative to the current mid,
a slightly stale forecast is ``the move still expected from now''.  The features enter
the GRU and the direct path like the lookback features.

\subsection{Training, validation and test periods}
\label{sec:data:splits}

The twenty-one days are split by date.  Models are trained on 6--12 March, and every
design choice, hyper-parameter and number of \cref{ch:experiments} is taken on the
validation day, 13 March; the period 24--26 March is a locked test set, never evaluated
and never inspected, reserved for the final numbers of the thesis
(\cref{sec:horizons:test}).  The exploratory analysis of \cref{ch:exploration} obeys the
same split: the price process and the feature space are read on the training and
validation days only, and the one part that uses no label and no model, the description
of the transaction graph, covers the whole window.  The evaluation protocol that goes with
the split, the metric, the subsets, the bootstrap intervals and the calibration rule, is
given with the experimental setup (\cref{sec:data:protocol}).

\paragraph{Why the periods are not contiguous.}  The days 14--23 March separate the
validation day from the test period.  The reason is the cost of the stateful runs.  Every
stateful model streams its training days in time order, once or twice, at about two hours
per day on a shared GPU, and the study has dozens of such runs: the training week was
therefore fixed at 6--12 March at the start, and the engineered features, the tree
log-probabilities and the forecast caches that the models read were built for the same
days.  The test period was fixed at the last three days of the window, 24--26 March, when
the dataset was assembled and before any model was trained, with the three days before it,
21--23 March, reserved as the warm-up through which a saved model rebuilds its states
before it is scored (\cref{sec:horizons:test}).  The days 14--20 March are used by
nothing: no model is trained, validated or tested on them, and the dip of 20 March
(\cref{sec:data:quality}) falls inside this unused stretch.  The gap has a cost, measured in
\cref{app:results:lag}: a model fitted on 6--12 March meets the test
period twelve days after its last training day, so any drift of the regularities over
those days appears as a loss on the test days relative to the validation day.
\Cref{sec:horizons:test} compares the two, and \cref{sec:concl:limits} discusses what a
contiguous protocol, training up to the warm-up days and calibrating on them, would
change.

\section{Exploratory analysis}
\label{ch:exploration}

Before training anything, a series of plain questions about the data was answered, each
with one picture.  The questions go from the smallest unit of the data, a single
order-book event, to the most abstract, the geometry of the feature space, and every
answer narrows what a model can be expected to do.  The section has three parts.
\Cref{sec:expl:price} is about the \emph{price process}: what the raw stream looks like,
how often the price moves and whether that depends on how far ahead one looks, where the
price sits, when it moves and which way, and whether the kind of contract, the related
contracts or the traders say anything about it.  \Cref{sec:expl:graph} is about the
\emph{transaction graph}: who trades what, how concentrated and how connected the trading
is, and how the graph is organised.  \Cref{ch:structure} is about the \emph{feature
space}: how many different things the features measure, what organises the space, how
much of the label simple models can read and which features carry the information,
whether the markets form families by how they trade, and how all of this changes with the
horizon.  Each subsection states its question, shows a figure and ends with the answer.
The numbers of the first and third parts are computed on the training and validation days
of \cref{sec:data:splits}, never on the locked test period; the second part uses no label
and reads the whole window.  These are exploratory findings: they say which regularities
the data have, and \cref{ch:models} to \cref{ch:horizons} are where the models are held
to them.  \Cref{sec:expl:limits} says what the analysis cannot say, and
\cref{sec:expl:agenda} collects what it says.

\subsection{What this analysis cannot say}
\label{sec:expl:limits}

Four limits bound everything above.  The data are three weeks of one venue, 6--26 March
2026: the shapes of the figures, the activity clock, the U-shaped distribution of the
price level, the symmetric lead--lag curve, are the kind of regularity that survives a
change of month; the exact shares and lifts are not, and nothing here says how they would
look in an election week or in a quiet August.  The feature-space study reads one
validation day and the price-process numbers the training and validation days; the locked
test days are untouched, which is the point, but it also means that every finding of this
section is a hypothesis about the test days, not a result on them.  The samples are
dominated by the busy markets, ten million samples a day from the sixteen thousand markets
that have a labelled sample,
most of them consecutive samples of the same few hundred books; the deduplicated view is
the counterweight, and a number that holds on all samples and not on the deduplicated ones
is a number about the busy markets only.  Finally, co-movement is not causation: that
siblings move together, or that skilled wallets exist, says what the data show and not
what makes a price move; and the categories and templates used throughout are derived
from tags and from masked question texts, convenient labels rather than ground truth.

\subsection{What the data say before a model is trained}
\label{sec:structure:agenda}
\label{sec:expl:agenda}

Five findings carry over to the methodology and the experiments; together they are a
first answer to \ref{q:process}.

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*, itemsep=3pt]
\item \textbf{Whether comes before which way.}  Below one hour the main question is
whether the price will move at all: at one minute the direction of a move is close to a
coin toss, while at twelve hours the direction is the whole task.  The balance of the classes, and with it the
calibration of every classifier, changes with the horizon.
\item \textbf{The activity clock is the first thing to know; the price level and the kind
of contract condition everything.}  How long ago the price last moved and how often it
moved in the last hour predict whether it will move again; the price level bounds what it
can do; the template of the question sets how often it does it.
\item \textbf{The direction is weak, and it lives in the first level of the book and in
the reversal of the recent change; the neighbours add nothing.}  The best single quantity
is right 57\% of the time at one hour; the contracts of the same event move in the same
minute and nobody leads; the skill of the traders, though real, is already in the book by
the time a model reads it.
\item \textbf{The graph is one piece with a dense core, a long tail of sparse wallets and
an organisation that the metadata already encode.}  A model with a state per wallet finds
most states empty or stale, and a message-passing model finds no locality to exploit; the
expectation for the graph models is a negative one.
\item \textbf{The features are seventeen quantities read several times, organised by
market and activity rather than by the label.}  The readable part of the label is
``whether'', which is linear and local; the direction needs thresholds and interactions,
which is where the boosted trees earn their margin; and the information moves from the
activity clock to the shape of the book as the horizon grows.  The families of markets
defined by how they trade are not the categories: the live-game regime, not the sports
category, is the hard case.
\end{enumerate}

For the model families these findings set the expectations.  A model that reads the
activity clock, the first level of the book, the long returns, the price level and the
contract type already has access to most of the information the data contain; the
stateless control of \cref{ch:stateless} is such a model.  For the stateful family the
open question is whether a state per market adds anything to that summary, and the
organisation of the feature space by market suggests that it may, most of all at the
shortest horizon.  For the graph family the question is whether a neighbourhood adds
anything, and the structure of the graph suggests that it will not.  Finally, every model
should be assessed by regime and by price level, not only by category.
