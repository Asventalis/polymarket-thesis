\chapter[The Dataset]{The Dataset: Collection, Preparation and Exploration}
\label{ch:data}

This chapter follows the data from their three sources to the objects the models read, in
the order of the pipeline: what was collected and from where (\cref{sec:data:sources,sec:data:collection}),
how large and how reliable it is (\cref{sec:data:scale,sec:data:quality}), how the raw
streams were preprocessed into a common time axis and per-block tensors (\cref{ch:prep}),
and how the final dataset, its labels, samples, features and periods, was defined
(\cref{sec:data:final}).  It closes with an exploratory and statistical analysis of the
data (\cref{ch:exploration}): the price process, the transaction graph and the feature
space, read with the tools of exploratory analysis and classical data mining before any
model is trained.

\section{The four tables}
\label{sec:data:sources}

The dataset covers twenty-one consecutive days, from 6 to 26 March 2026, and consists of
four tables (\cref{tab:data:sources}).  The \emph{markets} table describes every market
that existed in the period: question text, tags, the two outcome tokens, creation and
end dates, and the final outcome.  The \emph{order book} table is the stream of
price-level updates of the ``Yes'' side of every market's book: each row carries the
best bid and ask, the changed price level, size and side, and the mid price, with two
timestamps (creation and reception).  The \emph{snapshots} table holds full order-book
states.  The \emph{transactions} table is the on-chain record of every fill: block
number, timestamp, transaction hash, wallet, role (maker or taker), token, side, quantity,
price and fee.

\section{Collection}
\label{sec:data:collection}

The three streams come from three places.

\subsection{Market metadata: the Gamma API}

The \emph{markets} table was built from Polymarket's public metadata service (the Gamma
API): the markets closed in the window were paged through its keyset endpoint, ordered by
start date, with the tag list attached, and the markets that appear in the transactions but
not in that listing (tokens traded in the window on markets created outside it) were
completed one by one through the order-book operator's markets-by-token endpoint.  The tags
are the source of the eight categories used throughout the thesis (crypto, sports, esports,
politics, weather, economy and tech, culture, other), assigned by a fixed mapping from the
tag list.

\subsection{The order-book archive}

The \emph{order book} and \emph{snapshots} tables come from a public archive of the
operator's websocket feed, the \emph{Polymarket Tick-Level Orderbook Dataset} published on
Kaggle by Marvin Gozo under a CC~BY-NC~4.0 licence \cite{gozo2026polymarket}: one file per
day, restricted to the Yes side of every book.  Every row
of the feed carries two timestamps: the time at which the operator created the message and
the time at which the archive's collector received it; the thesis uses the creation time as
the time of the event and keeps the reception time only to check the feed's latency.

\subsection{The on-chain transactions: a blockchain scraper}

I collected the \emph{transactions} table with a modified version of
\path{poly-trade-scan} \cite{martkir2026polytradescan}, an open-source Python client that
connects to a Polygon node over a WebSocket JSON-RPC endpoint, reads every block, filters
the transactions addressed to Polymarket's exchange contracts and decodes their
\texttt{matchOrders} calls.  The original tool emits one row per transaction, the taker's
order with its nominal amounts; my version decodes the whole call, the amounts actually
filled and received by the taker, the filled amount and the fee of every maker order, and
emits one row per wallet and per fill (block number, timestamp, transaction hash, order
index, wallet, role, token, side, quantity, price, total collateral and fee rate), with
the longer timeouts and the many retries that a free public node requires.  The node was
the public endpoint of dRPC (\path{wss://polygon.drpc.org}); the token identifier of
every fill is then mapped to its market through the markets table.  A transaction with one taker and $n$ makers yields $n$ taker rows and $n$
maker rows, so the 219 million rows are \emph{fill legs}; 79 million of them are taker legs.

\section{Scale, coverage and storage}
\label{sec:data:scale}

Daily activity is stable over the period: 9.2--11.8 million transaction rows,
122{,}000--161{,}000 distinct wallets and 15{,}000--21{,}000 active markets per day.
Markets and wallets are mapped to dense indices (175{,}292 markets, 646{,}394 wallets).
The data were stored as Parquet files and queried with DuckDB
\cite{raasveldt2019duckdb}; all derived tensors were produced with SQL and NumPy and
checked by unit tests.  The raw files are never modified and every derived dataset is
rebuilt from them (\cref{app:reproduction}).

\section{Data quality}
\label{sec:data:quality}

Four properties of the data shape every later decision.

\paragraph{Fabricated mid on one-sided books.}  When one side of the book is empty, the
feature builder substitutes $0.49$ or $0.51$ for the missing price; 2.4\% of the rows have a
fabricated mid and 2.1\% a crossed book.  Excluding those samples changes the headline
metrics by less than $0.2$ points, so they were kept, but the thesis flags them.

\paragraph{Token orientation.}  The order book, and therefore the label, refers to
token~1 only in Yes/No markets and to token~2 in all other markets (Up/Down, Over/Under,
team~A/team~B), whereas the raw transaction direction is defined with respect to token~1
everywhere.  Without a correction, the sign of a buy is wrong relative to the label in 82\%
of the edges of a typical day (8.5\% of the labelled 1h samples).  Every model reads the
corrected sign (\cref{sec:models:mp}).

\paragraph{Price level.}  About 40\% of the samples have a mid below $0.05$ or above
$0.95$ (\cref{tab:expl:classes}); their dynamics are those of a bounded, one-tick process
and they are where every model struggles most.  Every result is therefore reported
separately on the extreme and the central samples (\cref{sec:data:protocol}).

\paragraph{Gaps.}  The two streams have one dip in common.  Between 01:00 and 03:00 UTC
on 20 March the hourly number of book events falls from about nine million to 1.3
million and the hourly number of fill legs from about 500 thousand to 79 thousand, and
both recover within three hours (\cref{fig:graph:summary}).  The two streams are
collected independently, by the archive's collector and by my node scraper, so the dip
is in the activity of the venue, not in the collection; Polymarket's public status
history lists no incident for that day.  No model is trained or evaluated on 20 March.

\section{Preprocessing}
\label{ch:prep}

The data of \cref{sec:data:sources} are three streams on three clocks.  This section turns
them into the objects the models read: a common time axis and per-block tensors.

\subsection{Time axis: blocks and the synchronisation of the two streams}
\label{sec:data:blocks}

Polygon produces a block roughly every two seconds, and every transaction carries its
block number.  The block is therefore the natural time step of the on-chain stream and
it is also the time step of the models: a ``step'' is one block, the set of transactions
in it, and the order-book updates that arrived since the previous block.  About 1{,}800
blocks per hour contain Polymarket transactions.  Order-book updates arrive at their own
times; a model that acts at block $t$ sees every update with a timestamp strictly before
$t$.  Because the two
streams are collected by different clocks (\cref{sec:data:sources}), every stateful model
reads the book updates created strictly before a block time and the fills of the block
itself; a sample at block $t$ never sees an update created at or after $t$.

\subsection{Derived tensors}
\label{sec:data:tensors}

\paragraph{Order-book features.}  For every market and every order-book update, a
52-dimensional feature vector is computed from the book state at that moment: best bid
and ask, five levels of bid and ask prices and sizes, spread, mid price, volume and
depth imbalances, recent trade statistics, and rolling statistics of the mid.  The mid
price is stored in column~40 of the vector; column~0 is the relative deviation of the
best bid from the mid.  The vectors are stored as hourly Parquet partitions (about
287{,}000 snapshots per hour, 27~GB in total).  To these, the models append the
long-lookback price features of \cref{sec:models:lookback}.  The order-flow imbalance
features follow \textcite{cont2014price}.


\paragraph{Market lifecycle.}  A table of 283{,}318 open/close events gives, for every
market, the time at which its contract was deployed (known in advance, hence causal)
and the time of its last trade (known only in hindsight).  The deployment time defines
when a market may enter the model.  The last-trade time is used only to define the
evaluation sample (a label is not computed when the market stops trading before the
horizon), never as an input.

\paragraph{Static market features.}  Each market has a 390-dimensional static vector:
a 384-dimensional sentence embedding of its question text, produced by a frozen
MiniLM sentence encoder \cite{reimers2019sbert}, and six numerical fields (initial
prices, creation date).  These features are fixed at market creation, so they are
leak-free; they initialise the market's state (\cref{sec:models:state}) through a fixed
random projection.  What the text contributes to prediction is measured in
\cref{sec:analysis:nostate}.

\paragraph{Identifiers.}  Markets and wallets are mapped to dense indices
(175{,}292 markets, 646{,}394 wallets) so that their states can live in two matrices on
the GPU.

\section{The final dataset: labels, samples, features and periods}
\label{sec:data:final}

This section defines the dataset on which every model of the thesis is trained and
evaluated: the label and the sample population, the hand-made features and the other
feature tables, and the split of the twenty-one days into training, validation and test
periods.

\subsection{Labels}
\label{sec:data:labels}

The target of a market $m$ at block time $t$ and horizon $H$ is the direction of the
change of its mid price between $t$ and $t + H$ (\cref{sec:bg:notation}):
\begin{equation}
  y_{m,t} =
  \begin{cases}
    \textsc{up}    & \text{if } \operatorname{mid}_m(t + H) > \operatorname{mid}_m(t), \\
    \textsc{const} & \text{if } \operatorname{mid}_m(t + H) = \operatorname{mid}_m(t), \\
    \textsc{down}  & \text{if } \operatorname{mid}_m(t + H) < \operatorname{mid}_m(t),
  \end{cases}
  \label{eq:label}
\end{equation}
where $\operatorname{mid}_m(s)$ is the mid price of the last order-book update of $m$
strictly before $s$.  A price is \textsc{const} when the two mids are \emph{exactly}
equal: there is no tolerance band, the mid moves in ticks of $0.001$, and a move of one
tick or more in either direction is \textsc{up} or \textsc{down}.  Three choices matter:

\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item \textbf{Three classes.}  The price is a step function with ticks of $0.001$;
  over one hour the mid of more than half of the samples does not move at all
  (\cref{tab:expl:classes}).  Collapsing ``no change'' into one of the two directions
  would make the task ill-posed; the three-class formulation keeps it well posed.
  \item \textbf{The last update before $t+H$, wherever it is.}  The update that defines
  $\operatorname{mid}_m(t+H)$ can live in any hour between $t$ and $t+H$, so the lookup
  scans every hour of the interval: a market that moved in between and was quiet
  afterwards is labelled by that move, not \textsc{const}.
  \item \textbf{Unknown is unknown.}  If $t+H$ is beyond the loaded data, the label is
  missing, not ``whatever the last price was''.
\end{itemize}


\paragraph{Samples.}  A sample is a (market, block) pair $(m, t)$ in which the market
received at least one order-book update in the block (\cref{sec:bg:notation}): a
prediction is asked whenever something new about a market is observed, and a market whose
book did not change in a block has nothing new to read.  Every model is asked for a
prediction at every such pair, so markets that update often contribute many samples.  Because consecutive
samples of the same market within a few seconds are almost identical, every result is
also reported on a \emph{deduplicated} subset that keeps at most one sample per market
per 15 minutes.  At the 6h horizon the validation day has 9.3 million samples, 1.2
million after deduplication.

\subsection{Feature engineering}
\label{sec:prep:features}

\paragraph{The sixty-one hand-made features.}  For every validation sample of the 1h model (same time, market and label), and later for
every tensor row, 61 causal features were computed from the clean data: the mid, its changes over
15 minutes and 1, 3, 6 and 12 hours and their signs, and the hour (\emph{price history},
the inputs of the direct path); the spread, the level-1, 5-level and 10-level depth
imbalances, the microprice, the sizes and the number of filled levels (\emph{book
shape}); the order-flow accumulators, event counts in the last 1, 5, 15 and 60 minutes,
the time since the last snapshot and since the last mid change, the number of mid changes
and the realised volatility of the last hour, and the position of the mid in its one-hour
range (\emph{activity and clock}); the signed taker flow of the on-chain trades over 5, 15
and 60 minutes (\emph{trades}); and the category, the outcome type, the time to the end
date, the duration and the age of the market (\emph{static}).  The full list, with
the exact definition of each feature, is in \cref{app:features}.  These features feed the
stateless learners of \cref{sec:models:stateless} and, from \cref{sec:models:handinputs}
on, the stateful model as well; \cref{ch:structure} studies their structure before any
learner is trained on them.

\paragraph{Sibling features.}  The contracts of the same event form a \emph{market--market} graph; its information is
measured through ten causal \emph{sibling features} computed for every row from
the last rows of the market's siblings within six hours: how many are active, their mean
15-minute and 1-hour returns, the largest of the latter, the sum of their mid prices (the
consistency of the event), the time since the latest sibling move, their net trade flow and
order-flow sign.  Siblings are the contracts of the same game (from the market's slug) or of
the same question template and end date (the candidates of one election, the strikes of one
ladder, the two sides of one seat): 9{,}356 groups of two to sixty markets, covering 74\% of
the control's rows with about eleven active siblings each.  If the trees find nothing in these
features, a message-passing layer over the same edges has nothing to carry, and the
lead--lag statistic of \cref{sec:expl:siblings} says why.

\paragraph{News features.}  The \emph{news flow}: every market is mapped to the entities named in its question and tags
(proper-noun phrases with generic words removed; 129 thousand of the 175 thousand markets,
74\% of the validation samples), and the GDELT Global Knowledge Graph files, one every fifteen
minutes, give for each entity the number of articles naming it and their tone; twelve causal
features per row follow (counts over one, six and twenty-four hours, bursts against the
previous seven days, tone and its change, share of the news flow, time since the last
mention), computed from the files published at least ten minutes before the row.  Like the sibling features, the news features
are measured in the stateless learner first (\cref{sec:external:news}).

\paragraph{Wallet features.}  For the graph models, every transaction edge carries a
seven-dimensional feature (side, role, signed size, price, fee, token orientation) and, in
the enriched variants, a causal record of the wallet's past accuracy at the horizon
(``skill''), a placebo skill (another wallet's record), and the wallet's past behaviour
(activity, maker share, one-sidedness, size, breadth); \cref{sec:models:mp} says how they
enter.

\paragraph{Foundation-model features.}\label{sec:models:tsfm}  Eleven numbers are appended
to every window row, derived from a zero-shot Chronos-2 forecast \cite{ansari2025chronos2} of the market's one-minute mid series: the
10\%, 50\% and 90\% quantiles of the forecast at 15, 30 and 60 minutes ahead, expressed
as scaled differences from the current mid, the sign of the 60-minute median change,
and an availability flag.  Forecasts are computed on a five-minute grid from the
512 minutes of history strictly before the grid time, and a sample at time $t$ reads
the last forecast made at most eight minutes before $t$; relative to the current mid,
a slightly stale forecast is ``the move still expected from now''.  The features enter
the GRU and the direct path like the lookback features.

\subsection{Training, validation and test periods}
\label{sec:data:splits}

The twenty-one days are split by date.  Models are trained on 6--12 March, and every
design choice, hyper-parameter and number of \cref{ch:experiments} is taken on the

\section{Exploratory and statistical analysis}
\label{ch:exploration}

Before any model is trained, the data say a great deal about what can and cannot be
predicted.  This section reads the three streams with the tools of exploratory analysis
and classical data mining, in three parts.  The first (\cref{sec:expl:price}) is the
\emph{price process}: what the stream of book events looks like, how often a price moves
over each horizon, how the price level bounds the dynamics, how regular the activity of a
market is, what the kind of contract says, whether the contracts of the same event lead
one another, and whether the wallets that trade carry information.  The second
(\cref{sec:expl:graph}) is the \emph{transaction graph}: how large, how dense and how
connected the bipartite graph between wallets and markets is, how it grows, and how its
map is organised.  The third (\cref{ch:structure}) is the \emph{feature space}: how many
quantities the hand-made features of \cref{sec:prep:features} contain, what organises the
space they span, how much of the label is linear, local or made of thresholds, and which
families of markets emerge from how they trade.  Every number of the first and third parts
is computed on the training and validation days of \cref{sec:data:splits}, never on the
locked test period; the second part is purely descriptive, uses no label, and covers the
whole window.

\subsection{What the data say before a model is trained}
\label{sec:structure:agenda}
\label{sec:expl:agenda}

The findings of this chapter set the agenda of the methodology and the experiments and are, in the terms of
\cref{sec:intro:questions}, a first answer to the first research question.

\paragraph{The price process.}  Predictability at short horizons is first of all
\emph{whether} the price moves, and that is written in the market's own recent activity;
the direction, when it moves, is weakly in the first level of the book and in the reversal
of the recent change; the price level and the kind of contract condition everything; the
neighbours of a market, whether the wallets that trade it or the contracts of its event, do
not carry information that the market's own book does not already show.  A model that reads
the activity clock, the first level, the long returns together with the price level and the
contract type has most of what there is; the question for the stateful and graph families
is whether a state or a neighbourhood adds anything on top, and the answer turns out to
depend on the horizon.

\paragraph{The graph.}  The transaction graph is one connected component with a dense core
and a long tail of sparse wallets; its organisation is by category, contract type and date,
which the market's metadata already encode; a wallet's identity is often a single fill.
A model with a state per wallet will find most states empty, and a message-passing model
will find no locality.

\paragraph{The feature space.}  The features are about seventeen quantities read several
times, so the capacity of a learner is not the issue and redundancy is not a problem for
trees but is one for distance-based and linear methods.  The space is organised by market
and by activity, not by the label, which is why a per-market state has material to work
with and why deduplication is a necessary second view of every result.  The separable part
of the label is first of all \emph{whether} the price moves, which is linear and local; the
direction is neither, and it is where the boosted trees earn their margin over every
simpler learner and where, in \cref{ch:stateful}, the stateful model earns its margin over
the trees at the shortest horizon.  The information that the label asks of the features
moves with the horizon, from the activity clock at minutes to the shape of the book at
hours, and the learner that reads it best moves with it, from thresholds to linear
directions.  And the families of markets defined by how they trade are not the categories:
the live-game regime, not the sports category, is the hard case, and any model that is to
be judged by category should also be judged by regime.
