\chapter{Experiments and Results}
\label{ch:experiments}

This chapter reports the experiments.  \Cref{sec:data:protocol} gives the setup under
which every number is produced: the periods, the metric, the subsets, the intervals, the
calibration rule and the compute.  The families are then assessed in the order of
\cref{ch:models}, mostly at one hour, the horizon at which the design was done: causal
rules and stateless learners (\cref{ch:stateless}), the stateful model from its first
version to its last (\cref{ch:stateful}), the graph models (\cref{ch:graph}) and the
sources outside the book (\cref{ch:external}).  \Cref{ch:horizons} reads the same
families across the five horizons, by kind of market and for robustness;
\cref{sec:horizons:test} reserves the locked test set for the final numbers; and
\cref{sec:horizons:questions} discusses the results against the four questions of
\cref{sec:intro:questions}.

\section{Experimental setup}
\label{sec:data:protocol}
\label{sec:models:protocol}

Models are trained on 6--12 March and validated on 13 March, with 24--26 March locked
for the final numbers (\cref{sec:data:splits,sec:horizons:test}); hyper-parameters and
design choices were selected on the validation day.  All numbers in the thesis are balanced
accuracies \cite{brodersen2010balanced} on the validation day, unless stated otherwise.  Every
result is reported on all validation samples and on three subsets: the \emph{deduplicated}
samples (at most one per market per fifteen minutes, 1.2--1.3 million), the \emph{extreme}
ones (mid outside $[0.05, 0.95]$) and the \emph{central} ones.  Differences between models are
computed on exactly the same samples and carry a 95\% hour-block bootstrap interval in brackets
(\cref{sec:bg:eval}); ``MR'' is Mean Reversion-$H$.  Three rules make the comparisons fair.
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item \textbf{The same samples.}  The stateless learners are trained and evaluated on the
  samples of the stateful runs: at one hour the control was built from the run's predictions;
  at one and fifteen minutes it had to be rebuilt on the run's samples, because a control that
  samples tensor rows at the row time is a different population (27 of 598{,}872 rows in
  common at one minute; \cref{sec:horizons:short}).
  \item \textbf{The same calibration.}  The decision rule of a classifier trained with a
  balanced loss is not balanced-accuracy-optimal on a different class mix.  Every model is
  therefore given two logit offsets (on \textsc{down} and \textsc{up}) chosen on the even
  validation hours and applied to the odd ones and vice versa, on a grid of $\pm 0.6$ in steps
  of $0.1$ at one hour and $\pm 2$ in steps of $0.2$ at the short horizons, and models are
  compared calibrated against calibrated.  The offsets are worth $+0.3$ points to the trees at
  one hour and $+5.4$ at one minute; a comparison without them measures thresholds, not models.
  \item \textbf{Full-length runs.}  Where a short experiment was needed to screen many
  variants, it was trained on 11--12 March; every claim rests on full-length runs, and the
  cases in which a short test predicted the wrong sign are reported (\cref{sec:horizons:short-tests}).
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Compute and the order of the experiments.}  Every stateful run trains on one
GPU of a shared server, one run per GPU, for nine to seventeen hours depending on the
horizon and the load (\cref{app:runs}); the stateless learners train in minutes on a CPU.
Because of that cost the design was done at one hour, variants were screened with two-day
short tests and confirmed with full-length runs, the other horizons were run with the
final configurations only, and seeds were repeated where a gain was small.  Every run is
identified by a label that encodes its configuration and writes its validation
predictions to disk, so that any two runs can be compared on exactly the same samples
(\cref{app:runs}).

\section{Discussion: the four questions}
\label{sec:horizons:questions}

The results of this chapter, read together, answer the four questions of
\cref{sec:intro:questions} as follows.

\begin{description}[nosep]
  \item[\ref{q:process}, the process.]  Confirmed as stated in \cref{sec:expl:agenda}:
  the price is a bounded, discrete process whose most regular feature is its own activity
  clock, conditioned by the price level and the contract type; its trading is one connected
  graph organised by category, contract type and date, with a dense core and a long tail of
  one-fill wallets; its features are seventeen quantities organised by market and activity.
  \item[\ref{q:information}, the information.]  The market's own history, activity and
  first level carry nearly all of it; the long returns read with the price level and the
  lifecycle carry the long-horizon direction (\cref{sec:stateless:control}).  The wallets,
  the siblings, the news flow and the zero-shot forecasts add nothing reliable at one hour
  (\cref{ch:graph,ch:external}); the forecasts add a few tenths in the central price range
  on the full stream only.
  \item[\ref{q:models}, the models.]  Calibrated trees, a tabular network and the stateful
  model given the same inputs and the trees as a base tie at one hour and fifteen minutes;
  the online, time-ordered regime of the stateful model costs one to two points on its own;
  the graph never adds a reliable gain; at one minute the sequential state is worth $+0.6$
  alone and $+1.4$ as the residual of the trees, with intervals that exclude zero on every
  subset (\cref{sec:horizons:synthesis}).
  \item[\ref{q:horizon}, the horizon.]  It changes the task, from activity at one minute
  to a slow reversal at twelve hours, and the right family with it; the live-game regime and
  the crypto interval contracts are the hard cases at every horizon, because their price is
  driven by a reference the data do not contain (\cref{sec:horizons:where}).
\end{description}
