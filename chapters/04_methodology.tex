\chapter{Methodology}
\label{ch:models}

This chapter sets out the rationale of the study and the approaches that were compared.
\Cref{sec:meth:rationale} states the objective, the criteria by which an approach is
judged and the rules that decided which approaches were included and which were left
out.  \Crefrange{sec:models:baselines}{sec:models:external} then describe the five
families as they were built: causal rules on the price; stateless learners on hand-made
features; sequence models of the order-book stream, a continuous-time stateful model
built for this thesis, the same model given the trees as a base, and a pretrained
forecaster; graph models on the wallet--market and market--market graphs; and sources of
information outside the book.  Each family closes with its advantages and limits a
priori, and \cref{sec:models:summary} puts them side by side; what each did on the data
is the subject of \cref{ch:experiments}.  All of them read the inputs of
\cref{sec:data:final} and are evaluated under the protocol of \cref{sec:data:protocol}.

\section{Objective, criteria and rules of inclusion}
\label{sec:meth:rationale}

\subsection{Objective}
\label{sec:meth:objective}

The object of the study is the direction of the mid price of a Polymarket market over a
horizon $H$, read as the three-class label of \cref{sec:data:labels}, \textsc{down},
\textsc{const} or \textsc{up}, at every block in which the market updated its book, for
five horizons from one minute to twelve hours.  The task serves the four questions of
\cref{sec:intro:questions}: the exploratory analysis of \cref{ch:exploration} answers the
first, and the comparison of approaches built here is designed to answer the other three,
which publicly observable information predicts the direction, which families of models
can read it and what a sequential state or a neighbourhood is worth over a stateless
learner on the same inputs, and how the answers change with the horizon.  The task is the
prediction of the \emph{price while the market is open}, not of the outcome of the event;
the measure is balanced accuracy, because the three classes are of very unequal size and
change size with the horizon (\cref{sec:bg:eval}); and the unit of every comparison is
the paired difference between two models on the same samples, with an interval.

\subsection{Criteria of success}
\label{sec:meth:criteria}

A model is said to read information that another does not
when its balanced accuracy on the validation day exceeds the other's \emph{on the same
samples}, after the same post-hoc calibration, by a margin whose 95\% hour-block bootstrap
interval excludes zero, both on the full stream and on a deduplicated subset of independent
samples (\cref{sec:data:protocol}).  The reference competitor is first the best causal
rule found on the data, ``predict that the price will move back against its recent
change'', and then, once it exists, the best stateless learner on hand-made features.  The
final numbers on a locked test period are produced once, after every choice has been made
(\cref{sec:horizons:test}).  A difference whose interval includes zero is reported as a tie, and a
result that holds on one of the two views of the samples only, the full stream or the
deduplicated subset, is reported as such.

\subsection{Rules of inclusion and exclusion}
\label{sec:meth:rules}

Six rules decided which approaches were built and which were left out.

\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
  \item \textbf{Causal inputs only.}  An approach is admissible only if every input it
  reads at time $t$ is computed from data strictly before $t$ and every label from data
  after it.  Approaches that would need the outcome, the resolution time or the last trade
  of a market as an input are excluded; the last-trade time defines the sample population
  and is never an input (\cref{sec:data:tensors}).
  \item \textbf{Public information that the dataset contains.}  The inputs are the three
  streams and the public market metadata, plus two public external sources that could be
  aligned to them, the news flow of the GDELT project and a pretrained time-series
  forecaster.  External reference prices, the spot price of Bitcoin for the crypto
  contracts or the score of the game for in-play sports, are not in the data; their
  absence is measured in the results by kind of market (\cref{sec:horizons:where}), not
  modelled.
  \item \textbf{One model for all markets.}  Every learned approach is one model pooled
  over all markets, never one model per market: the predictive relation between a book
  and its next move is to a large extent universal (\cref{sec:bg:lob}), and most markets
  have too few samples of their own.
  \item \textbf{Every approach is measured against the best simpler one on the same
  inputs.}  A learned model is admitted only if it can be compared, on the same samples
  and with the same calibration, with the best causal rule and, once it exists, with the
  best stateless learner; where possible it contains the rule as a special case
  (\cref{sec:models:skip}), and where a deep model and a tabular learner read the same
  table, a second tabular learner is trained to separate the effect of the learner from
  that of the training regime.
  \item \textbf{Families that span the questions.}  The families were chosen so that each
  question of \cref{sec:intro:questions} has a paired comparison: a learner with a
  sequential state against one without, on the same inputs; the same stateful model with
  and without the transaction edges; the same stateless learner with and without each
  external source.  Approaches that would not add such a comparison are outside the
  scope: outcome forecasting with language models, the subject of most prior work on
  Polymarket (\cref{sec:pm:related}), trading strategies and their profitability, and
  per-market models.
  \item \textbf{A fixed compute budget.}  A full-length run of the stateful model takes
  nine to seventeen hours on one GPU of a shared server, one run per GPU
  (\cref{app:runs}).  Variants were therefore screened with two-day short tests and
  confirmed with full-length runs, every claim rests on the latter, hyper-parameters were
  set by a small number of screening runs rather than by a systematic search, and the
  combinations that the budget did not cover (the residual construction at fifteen
  minutes and at the long horizons, the hand-made features at six and twelve hours, a
  wallet memory at a slower time scale) are reported as not run rather than guessed.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Principles}
\label{sec:intro:principles}

Three methodological principles run through the thesis.

\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
  \item \textbf{Causality first.}  Every feature and every label is computed from data
  strictly before the prediction time, and the evaluation protocol is chronological.
  \item \textbf{Strong baselines, the same calibration for everybody.}  The reference
  competitor is not a random classifier but the best simple rule found on the data and,
  once it exists, the best stateless learner on the same inputs.  Every model is reported
  as a difference from the reference with a confidence interval obtained by resampling
  validation hours, and every model receives the same post-hoc calibration before it is
  compared, because at short horizons a decision threshold is worth more than a model.
  \item \textbf{Negative results are results.}  Several components that are reasonable
  a priori (the transaction graph, a mean-reversion initialisation, wallet-skill
  features, zero-shot foundation models, news features, sibling features) do not help,
  and the thesis reports them with the same care as the components that do, with the
  reason the data give for each.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Baselines: causal rules on the price}
\label{sec:models:baselines}

All baselines are causal rules on the mid price of the market itself.  With
$\Delta^{-}_{L} = \operatorname{mid}(t) - \operatorname{mid}(t-L)$ the change over the
past lookback $L$:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item \textbf{Constant}: always \textsc{const}.  Balanced accuracy $1/3$ by construction.
  \item \textbf{Momentum-$L$}: predict $\operatorname{sign}(\Delta^{-}_{L})$.
  \item \textbf{Mean Reversion-$L$}: predict $-\operatorname{sign}(\Delta^{-}_{L})$, and
  \textsc{const} if the price did not move.
\end{itemize}
With $L = H$, Mean Reversion is the strongest rule at every horizon
(\cref{tab:exp:baselines}); it is the reference of every comparison in the thesis.
Its strength has the reading of \cref{sec:expl:level}: on a bounded, discrete price most
moves are one-tick oscillations that revert, and a market that did not move in the last $H$
is likely not to move in the next $H$.

\Cref{fig:models:rule} draws the rule in the notation used for every model of this
chapter: dashed boxes are the inputs, orange boxes are computations or trained modules,
blue boxes are quantities kept or produced inside the model, green boxes are the output,
and every box states the size of what passes through it.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\input{figures/fig_models_rule}
\caption{Mean Reversion-$H$ as a block diagram.  Two numbers enter, one class comes out.}
\label{fig:models:rule}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Advantages and limits.}  A rule needs no training, cannot leak, costs nothing
and is readable; on a bounded, discrete price that mostly oscillates by one tick it is
also a strong competitor, which is why it is the first reference of every comparison.  It
reads one number: it cannot combine the recent change with the price level, the activity
of the market or the shape of the book, and it has no way to say \emph{whether} the price
will move other than by the price not having moved.

\section{Stateless learners on hand-made features}
\label{sec:models:stateless}

The strongest test of whether a recurrent state is where the information lives is a model
that has none.  The inputs are the sixty-one causal features of \cref{sec:prep:features},
in five groups: price history (the inputs of the direct path), book shape, activity and
clock, on-chain trades, and the static fields of the market.  A LightGBM classifier
\cite{ke2017lightgbm} with class-balanced weights was trained on one row in ten of
7--11 March and early-stopped on 12 March, adding one group at a time
(\cref{tab:exp:nostate}); the LightGBM parameters (learning rate 0.05, 63 leaves, 500
rows per leaf, feature and row subsampling 0.8, 600 rounds at most, early stopping on
12 March) are the same at every horizon.

On the same table, two neural tabular learners are trained as a tabular model should be
(\cref{sec:bg:tabular}): an MLP of three layers of 512 units on a piecewise-linear encoding
of every numeric feature (sixteen quantile bins), with shuffled mini-batches of 8{,}192 rows,
a one-cycle schedule over sixteen epochs and early stopping on 12 March, and the TabM
ensemble of sixteen members on the same backbone; both with the class-balanced
cross-entropy of the trees, three seeds each.  They serve one purpose: to tell whether a
difference between the trees and the stateful model is the learner or the training regime.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\resizebox{\textwidth}{!}{\input{figures/fig_models_stateless}}
\caption{The stateless learners as block diagrams.  (a) LightGBM: the 61 features of the
sample enter as they are, one tree per class is added at every round, and the three

\label{fig:models:stateless}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Reading the diagram (\cref{fig:models:stateless}).}  Both learners read the same
row: sixty numbers and the category of the market, which LightGBM treats as a categorical
column and the networks as eight indicators.  In LightGBM every round adds three trees, one
per class, each with at most 63 leaves and at least 500 rows per leaf; early stopping on
12 March chooses the number of rounds (536 at one hour, hence 1{,}608 trees; 324 at one
minute, 280 at six hours, 260 at twelve), and the sum of the trees of a class is that
class's score.  A softmax turns the three scores into probabilities, two logit offsets fitted
on the validation day shift \textsc{down} and \textsc{up} against \textsc{const}, and the
largest probability is the answer.  The networks first expand the row: every number is cut
into sixteen quantile bins and encoded piecewise-linearly, the hour becomes a sine and a
cosine, the category eight indicators, and a flag marks each missing value, 674 numbers at
one hour.  The MLP is three layers of 512 units with SiLU activations and dropout 0.15 and a
linear head to three scores (0.87 million parameters).  TabM keeps the same body and runs
sixteen members through it, each with its own learned scaling of the input (initialised at
$\pm 1$) and its own head; the prediction is the mean of the sixteen softmax outputs (0.91
million parameters).  Both are trained on shuffled mini-batches of 8{,}192 rows for at most
sixteen epochs, with the class-balanced cross-entropy of the trees, and their output is
calibrated and read exactly as the trees' is.

\paragraph{Advantages and limits.}  A stateless learner reads whatever can be computed
at the sample time, including the quantities that a window of book events hides, the time
since the last move, the number of recent moves, the price level, the age of the market;
gradient-boosted trees handle thresholds and interactions between them without any
scaling, train in minutes on a CPU, and can be trained the way tabular models are best
trained, on shuffled rows with early stopping.  The family's limit is its blindness to
order: it sees a summary of the past and never the sequence of events that produced it,
and what is not computed by hand is not seen at all.  Its rows are also millions of
near-duplicates of one another, which is why every result is read on the deduplicated
samples as well.

\section{Sequence models of the order-book stream}
\label{sec:models:sequence}

The third family keeps a state per market, updated from the stream of book events, and
asks the state for the prediction.  Its main member is a continuous-time stateful model
built for this thesis; the family also contains the same model given the trees as a base,
and a pretrained time-series forecaster used without training.

\subsection{The continuous-time stateful model}
\label{sec:models:ctdgnn}

The model, called CT-DGNN in the code, processes the stream block by block.  At block
time $t$ it receives the markets that updated their book since the previous block, with
a window of their last $K$ book states ($K = 10$ in the reference configuration), and
the transactions of the block as
edges between wallets and markets.  It keeps a state vector of dimension $d = 128$ for
every market and every wallet.  One step has four parts
(\cref{fig:models:architecture}).

Dashed boxes are inputs of the step, blue boxes are states kept between steps, orange

\subsubsection{States and initialisation}
\label{sec:models:state}

Market states are initialised by a fixed random projection of the 390-dimensional
static features (\cref{sec:data:tensors}); wallet states start near zero.  The
projection is not trained: the states are detached at every truncation window, so a
gradient could only ever reach it through the first window.  States live outside the
model, in two matrices on the GPU, and are updated in place; a \emph{sparse} engine
keeps, for the nodes touched in the current window, an overlay of their new values and
the time of their last event, so that a step costs time proportional to the number of
active nodes rather than to the 820{,}000 nodes of the graph.

\subsubsection{Continuous-time decay}
\label{sec:models:decay}

Between two events a state decays as $\mathbf{h} \leftarrow e^{-r\,\Delta t}\,\mathbf{h}$,
with a single learned rate $r$ shared by all dimensions and node types
(\cref{sec:bg:notation}).  Because
$e^{-ra}e^{-rb} = e^{-r(a+b)}$, applying the decay lazily at a node's next event is
identical to applying it at every block.  In every trained model $r$ converges to about
$0.04$ per second, a half-life of 17~seconds: the fast state is, by its own choice, a
very short memory.  Two alternatives were tested: no decay, and a fixed multi-scale
decay with groups of dimensions at half-lives of one minute, one hour, one day and
infinity (\cref{sec:stateful:chain}).

\subsubsection{Order-book update}

For each active market the window of $K$ book states (each 52 features plus the
appended price features of \cref{sec:models:lookback}) is encoded by a GRU over the
$K$ snapshots; its last output is the input of a GRU cell that updates the market
state, followed by layer normalisation:
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{h}_m(t) = \operatorname{LN}\Bigl(\operatorname{GRUCell}\bigl(\phi(\mathbf{W}_{m,t}),\;
  e^{-r\,\Delta t}\,\mathbf{h}_m(t^{-})\bigr)\Bigr),
  \label{eq:state-update}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{W}_{m,t}$ is the window, $\phi$ the window encoder (the GRU here, or the
time-first encoder of the next paragraph), $t^{-}$ the time of the market's previous
event, $\Delta t = t - t^{-}$, and $\operatorname{LN}$ layer normalisation.  This is the
only part of the model that reads the order book, and it is the same in the graph and in
the LOB-only variants.

\subsubsection{Window encoders: space first, or time first}
\label{sec:models:encoders}

The GRU above reads the window one snapshot at a time: at every step it sees all 55
channels of one book state (the 52 features and three momentum columns computed
within the window) and condenses them into one vector, and only the sequence of these
condensed vectors is a time series.  Space is read before time.  The alternative
encoder, called \emph{time first} in the code, inverts the order, in the manner of the
channel-independent models of \cref{sec:bg:lob}.  Each channel $c$ of the window is
read as a univariate series $x_c \in \mathbb{R}^K$; it is normalised within the window
by subtracting its last value and dividing by its standard deviation over the real
snapshots (the zero-padded rows at the start of a young market's history are masked),
and mapped by one linear layer $\mathbb{R}^K \to \mathbb{R}^{32}$, shared by all
channels, to which a learned channel embedding and a projection of three scalars (the
last value, the log standard deviation, the fraction of real snapshots) are added.  The
55 channel tokens then pass through one multi-head self-attention layer over the
channels and a linear map to the state dimension; this vector replaces the last GRU
output as the input of the state-update cell.  The columns that are constant along the
window (lookback and forecast features, \cref{sec:models:lookback,sec:models:tsfm})
enter through a separate linear map from the last row.  Nothing downstream changes.

The shared time map means that the encoder learns one notion of ``shape of a recent
series'' for all 55 quantities and lets the attention decide which quantities matter
together; a GRU over 64 snapshots of 55 channels, by contrast, does not fit on the
available GPU memory with thousands of active markets and five-block truncated BPTT.
The experiments of \cref{sec:stateful:encoders} compare the reference GRU over 10
snapshots, the same GRU over 32 snapshots, and the time-first encoder over 64.

\subsubsection{Hierarchical memory}
\label{sec:models:memory}

The per-block state is a 17-second memory.  To give the model a longer view without
changing the fast dynamics, each market gets two additional states, a \emph{minute}
state and an \emph{hour} state, each with its own GRU cell.  Once every 60~seconds the
minute state reads the per-block state; once every 3600~seconds the hour state reads
the minute state:
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{h}^{\min}_m \leftarrow \operatorname{GRUCell}\bigl(\mathbf{h}_m, \mathbf{h}^{\min}_m\bigr)
  \ \text{every 60 s}, \qquad
  \mathbf{h}^{\mathrm{hr}}_m \leftarrow \operatorname{GRUCell}\bigl(\mathbf{h}^{\min}_m, \mathbf{h}^{\mathrm{hr}}_m\bigr)
  \ \text{every 3600 s}.
  \label{eq:memory}
\end{equation}  Refreshes are event-driven (a market's slow state is refreshed at
its first event after its period has elapsed) and each market has a random phase, so
that refreshes are spread over time.  Message passing and the book update only ever
touch the per-block state; the predictor reads the three states side by side.  This
is the clockwork principle \cite{koutnik2014clockwork} adapted to irregular events.

\subsubsection{Global state}

The predictor also reads the mean of the per-block states of all markets active in the
block, $\bar{\mathbf{h}}(t) = \frac{1}{|A_t|} \sum_{m \in A_t} \mathbf{h}_m(t)$ with $A_t$
the set of markets active in block $t$, a market-wide context in the spirit of the
virtual global node of \textcite{feldman2026navis}.

\subsubsection{Long-lookback price features and the direct path}
\label{sec:models:lookback}
\label{sec:models:skip}

The window of $K$ book states spans a few minutes.  To let the model see what the
baselines see, every row of the window is extended with, for each lookback
$L \in \{15\text{ min}, 1, 3, 6, 12\text{ h}\}$, three numbers: $10\,\Delta^{-}_{L}$,
$\operatorname{sign}(\Delta^{-}_{L})$, and a flag that is zero when the price $L$ ago is
not observable (market younger than $L$).  These features go through the GRU like the
others, and they also enter a \emph{direct path}: a linear layer from the fifteen
numbers (plus their five absolute signs) to the three logits, added to the predictor's
output.  The layer is initialised to zero, so at the start the model is exactly the one
without the path; but with weights $a$ on the sign of $\Delta^{-}_{H}$ and $-a$ on its
absolute value it reproduces Mean Reversion-$H$ exactly.  The model therefore
\emph{contains the heuristic it has to beat}, the recommendation of
\textcite{feldman2026navis}, and can learn how far to depart from it.  Initialising
the layer \emph{at} the heuristic instead of at zero was also tested
(\cref{sec:stateful:chain}).

\subsubsection{Foundation-model features}

The eleven forecast features of \cref{sec:models:tsfm} enter the window rows and the direct
path like the lookback features; the encoder reads them as constant columns from the last
row (\cref{sec:models:encoders}).

\subsubsection{Predictor and training}

The predictor is a two-layer MLP with dropout on the concatenation of the per-block,
minute, hour and global states; its output plus the direct path gives the three logits,
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{z}_{m,t} = \operatorname{MLP}\bigl([\mathbf{h}_m, \mathbf{h}^{\min}_m,
  \mathbf{h}^{\mathrm{hr}}_m, \bar{\mathbf{h}}, \mathbf{x}_{m,t}]\bigr) + \mathbf{D}\,\mathbf{u}_{m,t},
  \qquad
  \hat{\mathbf{p}}_{m,t} = \operatorname{softmax}(\mathbf{z}_{m,t} + \boldsymbol{\delta}),
  \label{eq:predictor}
\end{equation}
where $[\cdot]$ is concatenation, $\mathbf{x}_{m,t}$ are the hand-made features (present
only in the runs of \cref{sec:models:handinputs}), $\mathbf{u}_{m,t}$ collects the inputs
of the direct path (the fifteen lookback numbers, their five absolute signs and, when
present, the hand-made features), $\mathbf{D}$ is the zero-initialised matrix of the
direct path and $\boldsymbol{\delta}$ the calibration offset of \cref{sec:models:protocol}.  The loss is the focal loss with $\gamma = 2$ and class weights $2/1/2$ for
\textsc{down}/\textsc{const}/\textsc{up}; alternatives (automatic weights,
undersampling of \textsc{const}, regression of the signed change with a calibrated
threshold) are in \cref{sec:stateful:variants}.  Gradients flow through five consecutive
blocks (truncated BPTT), the optimiser is AdamW with learning rate $10^{-4}$, and one
pass over the seven training days is one epoch; the reference results use a single epoch,
and the \emph{two-pass} runs of \cref{sec:stateful:regime} make a second pass with the
states reset and the optimiser kept.  Every run writes its validation predictions and
probabilities to disk so that the models can be compared on exactly the same samples.

\subsubsection{Hand-made features as inputs of the stateful model}
\label{sec:models:handinputs}

The sixty-one features of \cref{sec:prep:features} can also be given to the stateful
model.  They are computed for every tensor row (117.7 million rows over 6--13 March) and, at
every block, the standardised features of the market's last row (69 columns once the category
is one-hot encoded and the time-dependent ones are recomputed at the block time) are
appended to the window.  They enter in three places: the window rows read by the encoder,
the zero-initialised direct path, and the predictor, which reads them next to the states so
that an interaction such as ``time since the last move $\times$ price level'' does not have
to survive the recurrent update.  Nothing else changes.

\subsubsection{What passes through the model, and the runs by name}
\label{sec:models:sizes}

\Cref{fig:models:architecture} carries the sizes of the two-pass model on the hand
features, the configuration of the main results.  At every block the model receives, for
each active market, a window of the last $K = 64$ stored book snapshots; every snapshot is
a row of 55 channels (ten levels of bid and ask prices and sizes, 40 numbers; the mid,
spread, best quotes, order-flow and depth statistics, 12 numbers; three momentum columns
computed within the window), and the same constant columns are appended to every row of
the window: the 15 lookback numbers of \cref{sec:models:lookback} and the 69 columns of
the hand-made features (the sixty-one features of \cref{app:features} with the hour as a
sine and a cosine, the category as eight indicators and an availability flag), 139 numbers
per row.  The window encoder condenses the $64 \times 139$ window into one vector of 128
numbers: the time-first encoder maps each of the 55 channel series to 32 numbers with one
shared linear layer, adds a learned embedding of the channel and a projection of its last
value, scale and fill, mixes the 55 tokens with one self-attention layer (4 heads, width
32) and projects the $55 \times 32$ result to 128, adding a linear map of the 84 constant
columns of the last row; the GRU encoder of the reference configuration reads the $K = 10$
rows one after the other with a hidden size of 128 and keeps its last output.  A linear
map and a GRU cell of size 128 turn that vector and the market's previous state into the
new per-block state, normalised by LayerNorm; the minute and hour states are two more
vectors of 128 numbers, each updated by its own GRU cell from the state one level below;
the global state is the mean of the per-block states of the markets active in the block,
128 numbers.  The predictor concatenates the four states and the 69 hand columns of the
last row, 581 numbers, and passes them through one hidden layer of 64 units (SiLU, dropout
0.1) to the three logits; the direct path is one linear layer from the 89 numbers of the
last row (15 lookback, their five absolute signs, 69 hand) to the same three logits,
initialised at zero and added.  The model has 0.60 million trained parameters (0.75 in the
checkpoint, 0.15 of which belong to the message-passing module that the LOB-only runs
build but never call); the states, two matrices of $175{,}292 \times 128$ and
$646{,}394 \times 128$ numbers, are not parameters.  \Cref{tab:models:variants} lists the
configurations that appear in the result tables, with the name each carries there and
the sizes that change.

\subsection{The stateful model as the residual of the trees}
\label{sec:models:residual}

The combination of two models that know different things suggests a trained one.  LightGBM
with the control's hyper-parameters is fitted on the 55 inputs that the stateful model
receives through the hand-made features, and its class log-probabilities are computed at every
tensor row of the eight days, \emph{cross-fitted by day}: the rows of a training day are
predicted by a model fitted on the other six training days, the rows of 13 March by the model
fitted on all seven, so that no row is ever predicted by trees that saw its day.  The stateful
model then receives the three log-probabilities of the last row before the block as three more
inputs and adds them to its output logits, in the manner of a boosting stage,
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{z}_{m,t} = \log \mathbf{p}^{\mathrm{tree}}_{m,t} + \operatorname{MLP}(\cdots) + \mathbf{D}\,\mathbf{u}_{m,t},
  \label{eq:residual}
\end{equation}
with the same MLP and direct path as in \cref{eq:predictor}: at initialisation it is the
tree model, and training moves it only where the state and the book history change the
answer.  The number that measures the state is then the gain over the tree base.  In
sizes, the residual run differs from the two-pass model by four columns in every window row
(the three log-probabilities and an availability flag), by 585 inputs to the predictor and
93 to the direct path instead of 581 and 89, and by the three log-probabilities added to the
three output logits.

\subsection{A pretrained forecaster used zero-shot}
\label{sec:models:zeroshot}

The last member of the family needs no training.  Chronos-2 \cite{ansari2025chronos2}, a
time-series foundation model (\cref{sec:bg:tsfm}), is given the last 512 mids of a
market on a one-minute grid (a five-minute grid at the longest horizon), ending at the
sample time, and asked for the quantiles of the mid at $t + H$.  The forecast is turned
into a decision in three ways: the sign of the median, the sign of the median with a dead
zone of half a tick for \textsc{const}, and the position of the current mid in the
forecast's quantile bands.  The same quantiles, computed for every active market on a
five-minute grid, are the eleven forecast features of \cref{sec:prep:features} that the
stateful model can read.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\resizebox{\textwidth}{!}{\input{figures/fig_models_chronos}}
\caption{The pretrained forecaster as a block diagram.  (a) Zero-shot: 512 mid prices
enter, nine quantiles of the mid at $t+H$ come out, and a rule turns them into a class.
(b) The same quantiles, computed on a five-minute grid for every active market, become
eleven inputs of the stateful model.}
\label{fig:models:chronos}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Reading the diagram (\cref{fig:models:chronos}).}  Nothing in this member is
trained: Chronos-2 is used with its published weights, on one series at a time, with a
context of 512 values and a forecast of $H$ steps ahead.  The half-tick dead zone is
$\pm 0.0005$ around the current mid, half of the tick of 0.001; the band rules answer
\textsc{up} when a lower quantile is above the current mid and \textsc{down} when an
upper one is below it.  As features, the three quantiles at three horizons are expressed
as ten times their distance from the current mid, so that they are on the scale of the
lookback changes, and a forecast is reused for at most eight minutes.

\paragraph{Advantages and limits.}  A model with a state reads the raw stream and can, in
principle, keep what a summary throws away: the order in which depth appeared and
disappeared, the pattern of the last minute of a quiet market; its memories at three rates
give it a view from seconds to hours, and the direct path lets it start from the rule it
has to beat.  It pays for this in three ways.  It is trained once through the stream, in
time order, with the gradient truncated to a few blocks, a regime under which the same
learner on the same table is known to do worse than when it is trained on shuffled rows
(\cref{sec:bg:tabular}); a full-length run costs hours on a GPU, so few variants can be
tried; and its window is on an event clock, so that it is never told how much wall-clock
time its last twenty events span unless the time is given to it as a feature.  The
pretrained forecaster has the opposite profile: no training and a long context, but
weights fitted to other domains, a per-window normalisation that treats a one-tick
oscillation as a signal, and no notion of ``no change''.

\section{Graph models}
\label{sec:models:graph}

The fourth family is the one the project started from: the bipartite graph between
wallets and markets, read by message passing at every block of the stateful model, and,
in a second view, the graph between the contracts of the same event.

\subsection{Message passing on transactions}
\label{sec:models:mp}

The transactions of the block form the set of edges $E_t$ (\cref{sec:bg:notation}), each
a fill $e = (w, m, t, \mathbf{a}_e)$ with seven attributes $\mathbf{a}_e \in \mathbb{R}^7$:
the logarithms of the collateral and of the number of tokens, the price, the fee rate,
buy or sell ($\pm 1$), the token orientation ($\pm 1$) and maker or taker.  Messages go
from wallets to markets and from markets to wallets through attention over the incident
edges \cite{velickovic2018gat}, and the receiving state is updated by a GRU cell.  For a
market $m$ with incoming edges $N_t(m)$, $w$ being the wallet of edge $e$,
\begin{align}
  \mathbf{e}_e &= \operatorname{MLP}_{e}(\mathbf{a}_e), \qquad
  \alpha_e = \operatorname{softmax}_{e \in N_t(m)}\Bigl(\operatorname{LeakyReLU}\bigl(
  \mathbf{q}^{\top}[\mathbf{h}_w, \mathbf{h}_m, \mathbf{e}_e]\bigr)\Bigr),
  \label{eq:mp-attention} \\
  \mathbf{h}_m &\leftarrow \operatorname{LN}\Bigl(\operatorname{GRUCell}\Bigl(
  \sum_{e \in N_t(m)} \alpha_e \, \tanh(\mathbf{V}\mathbf{e}_e) \odot \mathbf{U}\mathbf{h}_w,
  \; \mathbf{h}_m\Bigr)\Bigr),
  \label{eq:mp-update}
\end{align}
with $\mathbf{q} \in \mathbb{R}^{3d}$ and $\mathbf{U}, \mathbf{V} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$
trained, and $\odot$ the element-wise product; the same update, with the same weights,
then runs from markets to wallets.  The \emph{token orientation} attribute is $+1$ when
the traded token is the one the order book refers to and $-1$ otherwise
(\cref{sec:data:quality}), so that a buy of the No token counts as a sell of the Yes
token.  Several
edge enrichments were tested: a causal record of the wallet's past accuracy at the
horizon (``skill''), a placebo skill (another wallet's record), and the wallet's past
behaviour (activity, maker share, one-sidedness, size, breadth).  Setting
\texttt{--ablation} removes the edges entirely and gives the LOB-only model.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\resizebox{\textwidth}{!}{\input{figures/fig_models_graph}}
\caption{Message passing on the transactions of one block.  Each fill is an edge between
a wallet and a market with seven numbers; the receiving state is updated by a GRU cell,
first on the market side, then on the wallet side.}
\label{fig:models:graph}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Reading the diagram (\cref{fig:models:graph}).}  The seven numbers of an edge
(log collateral, log tokens, price, fee rate, buy or sell, the side of the traded token,
maker or taker) are mapped by two linear layers to an edge vector of 128 numbers.  For
every node that received edges in the block, one attention head scores each incoming edge
from the states of its two ends and the edge vector, normalises the scores over the node's
edges, and sums the messages, each the product of a gated edge vector, its attention
weight and a linear map of the sender's state.  A GRU cell of size 128 with LayerNorm
writes the sum into the receiving state.  The module runs twice per block with the same
weights, wallets to markets and then markets to wallets, and the updated market state is
the one the book update of \cref{fig:models:architecture} continues from.  The graph adds
0.15 million parameters to the model and a state of 128 numbers for each of the 646{,}394
wallets.

\subsection{The two graphs}
\label{sec:models:twographs}

Two graphs are considered.  The first is the bipartite \emph{wallet--market} transaction
graph of \cref{sec:models:mp}: the stateful model with its message passing active is the
graph model, and the same model with \texttt{--ablation} (no edges) is its LOB-only
counterpart, so that the contribution of the graph is a paired difference at every stage of
the design.  The second is the \emph{market--market} graph between the contracts of the same
event.  It was not built as a message-passing layer; its information was measured first in
the stateless learner, through the ten sibling features of \cref{sec:prep:features}.  If
the trees find nothing in those features, a message-passing layer over the same edges has
nothing to carry, and the lead--lag statistic of \cref{sec:expl:siblings} says why.

\paragraph{Advantages and limits.}  The graph is the only family that can use what makes
this dataset unusual, the identity of every counterparty: a wallet's history across
markets, its past accuracy, and the co-movement of the contracts of one event are visible
to no other family.  Because the graph variant and the LOB-only variant are the same model
with and without edges, its contribution is a paired difference at every stage of the
design.  Its limits are in the data more than in the method: most wallets trade once and
have no history to carry (\cref{sec:expl:graph}); a trade is a book event before it is an
edge, so the order-flow features see it first; the shared decay erases a wallet's state
within a minute of its last trade; and keeping a state for 820{,}000 nodes is what makes
the sparse engine of \cref{sec:models:state} necessary.

\section{Information outside the book}
\label{sec:models:external}

Three sources were tried, none of which changes the learner.  The \emph{foundation-model
forecasts} of \cref{sec:models:tsfm}, used zero-shot as a forecaster of the mid in their own
right and then as inputs of the stateful model.  The \emph{news flow} about the entities of
a market, as the twelve features of \cref{sec:prep:features}, measured in the stateless
learner.  And the \emph{text} of the market, as the sentence embedding that initialises the
market state (\cref{sec:models:state}) and as a category indicator among the static
features (\cref{sec:expl:templates}).

\paragraph{Advantages and limits.}  These sources are the only way to reach what moves a
price from outside the book, and each is measured first in the stateless learner, where
adding a group of features costs minutes rather than hours.  Their limits are coverage and
alignment: three samples in four have a market with a named entity, the news flow counts
articles rather than reading them, the forecaster is noise at extreme prices
(\cref{sec:bg:tsfm}), and the text enters through a frozen embedding and a category.
